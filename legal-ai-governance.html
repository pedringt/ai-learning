Legal AI Governance Opportunity Evaluation

I investigated whether law firms represented a worthwhile AI-consulting market. The research found a real governance gap, then exposed the harder commercial problem: the firms with the clearest structural need may be the least attractive clients to pursue one at a time.

The question

Should we pursue law firms as clients?

I directed research across bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor products, real firm policies, and firm-size operating realities to answer it.

Question
Should an AI consultancy pursue law firms as a client market?
Evidence
Bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor documentation (Harvey, LegalOn, CoCounsel, Juro), real firm AI policies, and firm-size adoption data.
Conclusion
The gap is real, but solo and small practices—the firms most structurally underserved—may not justify a consulting offering sold one relationship at a time.
How I worked with AI

I set the research question, directed each phase, and decided what deserved further investigation. Claude did the actual research: mapped the vendor and bar guidance, built the governance structure this evaluation is based on, ran it through a worked example, and pressure-tested the emerging market hypothesis. I used those findings to decide where to keep digging and where the opportunity case weakened.

Part 1 · Problem discovery

What is making AI adoption difficult for law firms?

Before judging the market, I first needed to understand whether the operational problem was real: where legal AI use was failing, what professional guidance actually required, and what governance or workflow gaps remained even when vendors supplied capable tools.

Is the problem real?

Three findings, each grounded independently.

  1. A review requirement is weak if the workflow can't show it happened.

    Nine of eleven published AI-sanctions cases in this sample trace to the same root cause: a review step existed on paper but was never actually enforced, including one case where the supervising attorney signed off without reading the filing. (A small, non-exhaustive sample, not a census of published cases, and case details are as reported by secondary legal-AI trackers, not independently re-verified against primary court records.) Bar rules require the review to actually happen; whether it should also be provable is my interpretation of what that means in practice, not something the rules themselves spell out.

  2. The visible failures cluster in high-consequence work.

    Litigation tasks were among the less frequently AI-assisted use cases in the adoption data I reviewed, but every case in this sanctions sample involved a filing submitted to a court. That doesn't prove litigation is disproportionately risky, the sanctions record itself only exists because litigation produces public orders; a bad AI-drafted contract clause or internal memo usually doesn't. It does show governance can't be prioritized by frequency of use alone. Low-frequency, high-consequence work may need the stronger controls.

  3. Confidentiality earns its own section.

    Every firm AI policy in my sample gave data confidentiality a dedicated section, and it maps directly to attorneys' existing obligation under bar Rule 1.6. It's one of the two most solidly evidenced pieces of this research, independent of anything else here.

Finding real problems doesn’t necessarily mean there’s a good consulting opportunity.

Once the problem was clearer, I shifted from “is there a real gap?” to “does that gap create a client market worth pursuing?”

Part 2 · Opportunity evaluation

Given those problems, are law firms worth pursuing?

I tested the commercial thesis against adoption evidence, firm structure, and the practical realities of who would own and buy this work. That is where the initial market framing weakened.

The firm-size reality that changed the verdict

Most solo and small firms don't have dedicated IT support.

ABA technology survey data shows solo attorneys overwhelmingly make their own technology decisions themselves, and internal tech support is rare at small firms, rising sharply only at larger ones. Everything up to that point had quietly assumed a firm with a named policy owner and a second reviewer to catch mistakes. That assumption doesn't hold for most of the addressable market, and it raises a harder question than how to serve them: whether firms that size are worth pursuing as clients at all, given what it would take to sell and support them one at a time.

Earlier framing

A dedicated legal-AI governance offering could serve law firms broadly as clients.

Corrected framing

Solo and small practices appear most structurally underserved, exactly the segment that's hardest to justify pursuing one relationship at a time.

Where I landed

Real gap. Do not propose an offering yet.

The operational problem is real: review that isn't provable, and a use-case/risk mismatch a generic governance layer would miss. But the firms that appear most structurally underserved, solo and small practices, are also the hardest to build a client relationship around, individually too small to justify the kind of engagement this work usually requires. My recommendation at this stage is not to propose an offering. A real problem does not automatically make a worthwhile client base, and the next evidence needs to test whether there is a reachable segment with both the need and the budget for consulting help.

What I ruled out: treating this as a single undifferentiated "law firms" market, assuming every firm has a governance owner and IT support to lean on, and assuming a real problem automatically means a worthwhile client to pursue.

Next test

The next useful evidence has to come from actual lawyers, not more desk research.

The research supports a real governance gap and a plausible buyer mismatch. It doesn't establish urgency, budget ownership, or willingness to pay, that only comes from people who'd actually use, manage, or buy something built for this segment.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical, a packaged offering, or proven buyer demand.

What this practiced

Directing research toward a real business question, separating operational need from client-market viability, and letting evidence narrow the opportunity instead of forcing an offering.

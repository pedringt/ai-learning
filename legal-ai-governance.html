Evaluating a Legal AI Opportunity

In this self-directed research exercise, not client work, I asked whether an AI consultancy should pursue law firms as a client market. I started with a broad hypothesis: law firms need help governing AI, and a governance offering could meet that need. My verdict is that the governance gap is real but the commercial case isn’t strong enough yet. I would do customer discovery before proposing an offering. No customer discovery has been done yet.

Secondary research only: court orders, bar guidance, vendor documentation, published firm policies and industry surveys. No lawyers or legal-ops staff were consulted.

How I tested the idea

I compared what legal AI vendors build with what bar guidance expects of lawyers, read sanctions cases involving AI-drafted filings, reviewed published firm AI policies, and looked at how firms are organized by size. Then I checked my own conclusions against their sources. Some didn’t survive. One part of my draft governance framework rested only on a vendor’s marketing claim, so I cut it. Those corrections shaped the recommendation as much as the findings did.

What held up

  1. Human review needs to be more than a sign-off.

    Inadequate human review came up repeatedly in the AI sanctions cases I read about. Two court orders, checked against the orders themselves, show it directly. In Mata v. Avianca (S.D.N.Y., 2023), lawyers filed opinions created by ChatGPT and kept standing by them after the court questioned whether they existed. In Wadsworth v. Walmart (D. Wyo., 2025), motions citing nine cases, eight of which did not exist, carried the e-signatures of two attorneys who had not reviewed them. Two cases are examples; they don’t show how common this is.

    ABA guidance makes clear that using AI does not remove a lawyer’s existing ethical responsibilities. Making review explicit and, where useful, recording that it happened is my governance response, not a rule-mandated workflow.

    The cases I reviewed highlighted failures in court filings, where mistakes can become part of the public record. That doesn’t establish that litigation is uniquely risky. Errors in contracts, internal research, or other legal work may be less visible. The distinction matters because governance decisions should consider the consequences of a failure, not just the failures that are easiest to observe.

  2. Confidentiality needs its own treatment.

    ABA Formal Opinion 512 lists confidentiality under Model Rule 1.6 among the duties that principally apply when lawyers use generative AI. A firm’s AI policy should address it directly rather than in passing.

What I corrected along the way

  1. A survey pattern isn’t proof of demand.

    I initially treated survey data linking written AI policies with greater AI adoption as evidence that governance could help drive adoption. When I revisited the source, I couldn’t establish cause and effect. Firms with more resources might be more likely both to adopt AI and to develop formal policies. I kept the pattern as a correlation and stopped treating it as evidence that small firms would pay for governance help.

  2. Most of the market can’t run the control I designed.

    My early thinking assumed a firm with a named policy owner and a second reviewer, the person who is supposed to catch what the author missed. ABA technology survey data shows that solo attorneys usually make technology decisions themselves. Internal tech support is rare at small firms and becomes much more common only at larger ones. For a solo lawyer, “someone else checks the citations” becomes self-checking, which is a weaker control. This also raised a harder commercial question: are firms that size worth pursuing, given the cost of selling to and supporting them one at a time?

Where I landed

Real gap. Do not propose an offering yet.

The operational problem is real: review is hard to prove, and a generic governance layer could miss the difference between low-risk and high-risk work. The commercial case isn’t strong enough yet. I would not propose an offering until direct customer discovery shows that a reachable segment has both the need and the budget for consulting help.

What I ruled out: treating “law firms” as one market, treating a real problem as proof of a viable client base, treating a survey correlation as buyer demand, and assuming every firm has dedicated governance and IT owners. Solo and small practices appear most structurally underserved, but they are also the hardest segment to justify pursuing one relationship at a time.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical or proven buyer demand.

Sources

The two court orders were read directly. The other sanctions cases came from secondary legal-AI trackers and weren’t checked against court records, so they aren’t cited. The firm policies I reviewed aren’t cited either; the confidentiality point rests on the ABA’s published guidance. The survey correlation in “What I corrected along the way” comes from Clio’s 2026 mid-sized firms report. It is self-reported survey data and does not test cause and effect. I first encountered it through a secondary summary, so I cite the report itself. Vendor documentation (Harvey, LegalOn, CoCounsel, Juro) informed the research, but no conclusion on this page depends on it.

Legal AI Governance Opportunity Evaluation

The premise: an AI consultancy, aware I have a law degree, asked me to find out whether law firms were worth pursuing as a client market. I researched the actual gap between what AI vendors build and what bar rules require, and let a correction partway through change the answer.

The ask

Should we pursue law firms as clients?

That was the real starting question, not a governance hypothesis I invented going in. I researched bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor products, and real firm policies to answer it, and a correction partway through changed who the actual buyer would be.

Question
Given a law degree, should an AI consultancy pursue law firms as a client market?
Evidence
Bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor documentation (Harvey, LegalOn, CoCounsel, Juro), real firm AI policies, and firm-size adoption data.
Conclusion
There's a real, specific gap, but not with the firms a typical legal-AI governance effort assumes.
How I worked with AI

I set the research question, directed each phase, and decided which findings held up and which needed correcting. Claude did the actual research: mapped the vendor and bar guidance, built the governance structure this evaluation is based on, ran it through a worked example, and pressure-tested its own claims, including walking back one finding from causal to correlational once traced to its actual source, and cutting a section that turned out to rest on a single vendor's marketing line rather than real evidence.

What the research found

Four findings, one that changed the verdict.

  1. "Review" isn't a control unless it's provable.

    Nine of eleven published AI-sanctions cases reviewed trace to the same root cause: a review step existed on paper but was never actually enforced, including one case where the supervising attorney had signed off without reading the filing. (Case details as reported by secondary legal-AI trackers, not independently re-verified against primary court records.) A policy that says a human reviews this isn't the same as one that proves it happened.

  2. Usage and risk don't track together.

    The most common AI use cases in law are the lowest-stakes ones. Litigation work, the least frequently AI-assisted use case in this research, accounted for 100% of the sanctions cases found. A governance effort built around the most common use case would under-protect exactly the one causing real damage.

  3. Confidentiality earns its own section.

    Every real firm AI policy reviewed gave data confidentiality a dedicated section, and it maps directly to attorneys' existing obligation under bar Rule 1.6. It's one of the two most solidly evidenced pieces of this research, independent of anything else here.

  4. The correction that changed the verdict: most firms don't have an IT department.

    Roughly 70% of practicing lawyers are solo or at firms of ten people or fewer, with no dedicated IT or compliance function. Everything up to that point had quietly assumed a firm with a named policy owner and a second reviewer to catch mistakes. That assumption doesn't hold for most of the addressable market, and it's also where the actual opening is: the segment most legal-AI governance efforts, including the vendor products this research started from, are built for is the minority of firms, not the majority.

Catching a claim before it shipped

One finding didn't survive its own fact-check.

An early version of this research claimed firm governance directly "drives" the AI adoption gap between small and mid-sized firms. Tracing that claim past a secondary summary to its actual source, a Clio industry survey, showed it was self-reported, correlational data, not a controlled study. The claim was walked back to what the evidence actually supports: firms with written AI policies show meaningfully higher rates of extensive AI use, a pattern, not a proven cause. A separate piece of the proposed governance structure, an "institutional memory" section, was cut outright after a deliberate pressure test found its only real support was a single vendor's marketing line, thinner evidence than every other piece here, each of which ties to an actual bar rule, a named sanctions case, or a real firm policy.

Where I landed

Yes, there's a gap, just not with the firms a typical governance effort assumes.

The operational problem is real and specific: review that isn't provable, and a use-case/risk mismatch a generic governance layer would miss. But the segment with the clearest need, solo and small firms, roughly 70% of practicing lawyers, isn't who existing legal-AI governance efforts, including the vendor products this research started from, are built for. That's a genuine answer to the original question, not an inconclusive one: pursuing this market would mean designing for a buyer nobody else in the space is currently serving, a different starting point than the original framing assumed.

Earlier framing

A dedicated legal-AI governance offering could serve law firms broadly as clients.

Corrected framing

The firms most exposed to this gap are the ones current governance efforts, including this research's own starting assumptions, aren't built to serve.

What I ruled out: treating this as a single undifferentiated "law firms" market, assuming every firm has a governance owner and IT support to lean on, and stating the adoption-gap finding as causal once its actual source didn't support that.

Next test

The next useful evidence has to come from actual lawyers, not more desk research.

This research establishes that the gap and the buyer mismatch are real. It doesn't establish urgency, budget ownership, or willingness to pay, that only comes from people who'd actually use, manage, or buy something built for this segment.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical, a packaged offering, or proven buyer demand.

What this practiced

Directing research toward a real business question, catching a claim that didn't survive its own source-check, and letting a correction change the verdict instead of the framing.

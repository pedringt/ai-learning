Evaluating a Legal AI Opportunity

In this self-directed research exercise, not client work, I asked whether an AI consultancy should pursue law firms as a client market. My verdict: the governance gap is real, but the commercial case isn’t strong enough yet. Solo and small practices look the most underserved and are also the hardest to justify serving one relationship at a time, so I would do customer discovery before proposing an offering. No customer discovery has been done yet.

My role Research question, evidence standard, research direction, challenging assumptions & the recommendationAI collaborator Much of the source collection and synthesis, plus pressure-testing the market hypothesis (Claude)

What changed the recommendation

Three findings narrowed the opportunity.

  1. A review requirement is weak if the workflow can’t show it happened.

    Inadequate human review came up repeatedly in the AI-sanctions cases I read about. Two court orders, checked against the orders themselves, show it directly. In Mata v. Avianca (S.D.N.Y., 2023), lawyers filed opinions created by ChatGPT and kept standing by them after the court questioned whether they existed. In Wadsworth v. Walmart (D. Wyo., 2025), motions citing nine cases, eight of which did not exist, carried the e-signatures of two attorneys who had not reviewed them. Two cases are examples; they don’t show how common this is. ABA guidance makes clear that using AI does not remove a lawyer’s existing ethical responsibilities. Making review explicit and, where useful, recording that it happened is my governance response, not a rule-mandated workflow.

    Litigation was one of the least common AI use cases in the adoption data I reviewed, yet the sanctions cases I read about, including both orders above, involved court filings. That is more likely a visibility effect than proof that litigation is uniquely risky: court orders are public, and a bad AI-drafted clause or memo usually leaves no public trail. Either way, governance can’t be prioritized by how often something is used. Low-frequency, high-consequence work still needs the stronger controls.

  2. Confidentiality needs its own treatment.

    ABA Formal Opinion 512 lists confidentiality under Model Rule 1.6 among the duties that principally apply when lawyers use generative AI, so a firm’s AI policy should address it directly rather than in passing.

  3. Most solo and small firms don’t have dedicated IT support.

    ABA technology survey data shows that solo attorneys usually make technology decisions themselves. Internal tech support is rare at small firms and becomes much more common only at larger ones. My initial thinking had quietly assumed a firm with a named policy owner and a second reviewer to catch mistakes. That assumption does not hold for most of the addressable market, and it raises a harder question: are firms that size worth pursuing, given the cost of selling to and supporting them one at a time?

Where I landed

Real gap. Do not propose an offering yet.

The operational problem is real: review is hard to prove, and a generic governance layer could miss the difference between low-risk and high-risk work. The commercial case isn’t strong enough yet. I would not propose an offering until direct customer discovery shows that a reachable segment has both the need and the budget for consulting help.

What I ruled out: treating “law firms” as one market, treating a real problem as proof of a viable client base, and assuming every firm has dedicated governance and IT owners. My earlier framing, a legal-AI governance offering for law firms broadly, became a narrower one: solo and small practices appear most structurally underserved, but they are also the hardest segment to justify pursuing one relationship at a time.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical or proven buyer demand.

Sources

The two court orders were read directly. The other sanctions cases I originally looked at came from secondary legal-AI trackers, and that list wasn’t preserved. The firm policies I originally reviewed for the confidentiality point weren’t preserved either; that point now rests on the ABA’s published guidance (Formal Opinion 512 and Model Rule 1.6). I also reviewed vendor documentation (Harvey, LegalOn, CoCounsel, Juro) to compare the controls tools provide with what guidance expects, but no conclusion on this page depends on it, so those pages are not listed.

Legal AI Governance Opportunity Evaluation

The premise: an AI consultancy, aware I have a law degree, asked me to find out whether law firms were worth pursuing as a client market. I researched the actual gap between what AI vendors build and what bar rules require, and let a correction partway through change the answer.

The ask

Should we pursue law firms as clients?

I researched bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor products, and real firm policies to answer it, and a correction partway through changed who the actual buyer would be.

Question
Should an AI consultancy pursue law firms as a client market?
Evidence
Bar ethics guidance, published AI-sanctions cases, vendor documentation (Harvey, LegalOn, CoCounsel, Juro), real firm AI policies, and firm-size adoption data.
Conclusion
The gap is real, but the firms that need it most, solo and small practices, may not be worth pursuing as clients.
How I worked with AI

I set the research question, directed each phase, and decided which findings held up and which needed correcting. Claude did the actual research: mapped the vendor and bar guidance, built the governance structure this evaluation is based on, ran it through a worked example, and pressure-tested its own claims, including walking back one finding from causal to correlational once traced to its actual source, and cutting a section that turned out to rest on a single vendor's marketing line rather than real evidence.

Is the problem real?

Three findings, each grounded independently.

  1. A review requirement is weak if the workflow can't show it happened.

    Nine of eleven published AI-sanctions cases in this sample trace to the same root cause: a review step existed on paper but was never actually enforced, including one case where the supervising attorney signed off without reading the filing. (A small, non-exhaustive sample, not a census of published cases, and case details are as reported by secondary legal-AI trackers, not independently re-verified against primary court records.) Bar rules require the review to actually happen; whether it should also be provable is my interpretation of what that means in practice, not something the rules themselves spell out.

  2. The visible failures cluster in high-consequence work.

    Litigation tasks were among the less frequently AI-assisted use cases in the adoption data I reviewed, but every case in this sanctions sample involved a filing submitted to a court. That doesn't prove litigation is disproportionately risky, the sanctions record itself only exists because litigation produces public orders; a bad AI-drafted contract clause or internal memo usually doesn't. It does show governance can't be prioritized by frequency of use alone. Low-frequency, high-consequence work may need the stronger controls.

  3. Confidentiality earns its own section.

    Every firm AI policy in my sample gave data confidentiality a dedicated section, and it maps directly to attorneys' existing obligation under bar Rule 1.6. It's one of the two most solidly evidenced pieces of this research, independent of anything else here.

Checking the research's own claims

One finding didn't survive its own fact-check.

An early version of this research claimed firm governance directly "drives" the AI adoption gap between small and mid-sized firms. Tracing that claim past a secondary summary to its actual source, a Clio industry survey, showed it was self-reported, correlational data, not a controlled study. The claim was walked back to what the evidence actually supports: firms with written AI policies show meaningfully higher rates of extensive AI use, a pattern, not a proven cause. A separate piece of the proposed governance structure, an "institutional memory" section, was cut outright after a deliberate pressure test found its only real support was a single vendor's marketing line, thinner evidence than every other piece here, each of which ties to an actual bar rule, a named sanctions case, or a real firm policy.

The correction that changed the verdict

Most solo and small firms don't have dedicated IT support.

ABA technology survey data shows solo attorneys overwhelmingly make their own technology decisions themselves, and internal tech support is rare at small firms, rising sharply only at larger ones. Everything up to that point had quietly assumed a firm with a named policy owner and a second reviewer to catch mistakes. That assumption doesn't hold for most of the addressable market, and it raises a harder question than how to serve them: whether firms that size are worth pursuing as clients at all, given what it would take to sell and support them one at a time.

Earlier framing

A dedicated legal-AI governance offering could serve law firms broadly as clients.

Corrected framing

Solo and small practices appear most structurally underserved, exactly the segment that's hardest to justify pursuing one relationship at a time.

Where I landed

Real gap, but maybe not worth pursuing as clients.

The operational problem is real: review that isn't provable, and a use-case/risk mismatch a generic governance layer would miss. But the firms that appear most structurally underserved, solo and small practices, are also the hardest to build a client relationship around, individually too small to justify the kind of engagement this work usually requires. That's a genuine answer to the original question, not an inconclusive one: a real problem doesn't automatically make a worthwhile client base.

What I ruled out: treating this as a single undifferentiated "law firms" market, assuming every firm has a governance owner and IT support to lean on, stating the adoption-gap finding as causal once its actual source didn't support that, and assuming a real problem automatically means a worthwhile client to pursue.

Next test

The next useful evidence has to come from actual lawyers, not more desk research.

This research establishes that the gap and the buyer mismatch are real. It doesn't establish urgency, budget ownership, or willingness to pay, that only comes from people who'd actually use, manage, or buy something built for this segment.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical, a packaged offering, or proven buyer demand.

What this practiced

Directing research toward a real business question, catching a claim that didn't survive its own source-check, and letting a correction change the verdict instead of the framing.

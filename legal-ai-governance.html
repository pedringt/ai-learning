Evaluating a Legal AI Opportunity

I wanted to explore whether law firms could be a good market for AI consulting, particularly around how they manage AI risks. My research suggested there were real problems worth solving, but I wasn’t convinced that meant firms would pay for help solving them.

This was a self-directed research exercise, not client work. My conclusion was to investigate customer demand before developing an offering.

Secondary research only: court orders, bar guidance, vendor documentation, published firm policies and industry surveys. No lawyers or legal-ops staff were consulted. Research conducted August 2026; sources rechecked November 2026.

How I tested the idea

I approached the research with two questions: what problems are law firms actually facing with AI, and is there evidence they’d pay for help solving them?

I compared legal guidance, court cases, industry surveys, and existing products. When I checked my conclusions against the original sources, I found assumptions I needed to revise or remove.

What held up

  1. Human review needs to be more than a sign-off.

    The court cases I reviewed showed why human review needs to be more than a sign-off. In one case, lawyers submitted fabricated citations from ChatGPT. In another, fabricated citations came from a law firm’s own AI platform, and attorneys signed filings they hadn’t reviewed.

    Legal AI products are increasingly adding built-in verification features. Those may reduce some risks, but they don’t eliminate the need for lawyers to check the work. They also raise a business question: how much outside governance help would firms still need if their existing tools handle more of the verification?

  2. Confidentiality needs its own treatment.

    Accuracy wasn’t the only concern. Lawyers also have confidentiality obligations, which raise questions about what client information can be shared with AI tools and how that information is handled.

    I treated confidentiality as a separate governance requirement rather than assuming that reviewing AI-generated work would address it.

What I corrected along the way

  1. A survey pattern isn’t proof of demand.

    One of my early assumptions didn’t hold up. I’d interpreted higher AI adoption among firms with formal policies as evidence that governance could encourage adoption.

    Looking more closely, firm size and resources offered another explanation. I revised my conclusion: the data suggested a correlation, but it didn’t establish that governance caused adoption or that firms would pay for consulting.

  2. The approach didn’t fit smaller firms

    I realized I’d designed part of the governance process around the resources of a larger firm. It assumed there would be someone responsible for AI policies and another person available to check the work.

    That wasn’t realistic for many smaller practices. I needed to rethink how those safeguards would work with limited staff, while also considering whether smaller firms could support a viable consulting business.

Why I wouldn’t pursue an offering yet

I started with a plausible consulting idea and found evidence that the underlying problem was real. But as I examined the market, I became less convinced that a standalone governance offering was the right answer.

I’d need to learn how firms actually manage these risks, whether existing legal AI products already meet their needs, and what problems they’d pay someone else to solve. Without that evidence, I wouldn’t recommend moving forward.

Do next
Talk to a small group of solo or small-firm attorneys, or legal-ops people, about this specific gap.

Do not claim yet
A validated legal vertical or proven buyer demand.

NARC

NARC is a short satirical workplace-surveillance game about the gap between what a monitoring system can observe and what is actually true. I built it to make proxy metrics, missing context, and algorithmic management understandable through play instead of explanation alone.

The problem

A system can measure activity perfectly and still misunderstand the work.

Workplace monitoring becomes interesting when a proxy starts standing in for the thing an organization actually cares about. Presence can look like productivity. Fast responses can look like commitment. Quiet focus can look like inactivity.

I wanted to make that failure mode felt rather than simply describe it, so I turned it into a gameable system.

What I built

A fictional company laptop where visible behavior keeps colliding with real work.

Notice→Choose→Act→NARC interprets→Consequences

The player juggles messages, email, files, deadlines, coworker requests, and interruptions while NARC watches only what its fictional monitoring system can see. The game rewards learning the difference between the real goal and the signals used to judge it.

Visible activityThe proxy

NARC turns observable traces into a simplified picture of the employee.

Real workThe missing context

Important work can be quiet, slow, or invisible to the system measuring it.

CounterplayPeople adapt to measurement

Focus Time and the keepalive tool let the player see how metrics change behavior once people understand them.

Social choicesThe data is not neutral

Coworker reports, favors, and sabotage show how human incentives can enter an apparently objective system.

The key product decisions

The system had to be understandable enough to game and wrong enough to reveal the point.

Designed imperfectionNARC does not get complete context

The point is not an all-knowing AI making mistakes at random. Its conclusions come from partial evidence, so the player can learn why a plausible inference may still be wrong.

Comedy as explanationThe joke carries the concept

A mouse jiggler is funny, but it also makes the proxy problem concrete: once activity becomes the target, people optimize activity instead of the underlying work.

Short enough to finishOne busy day beat a larger simulation

I cut the concept down to a roughly 15-minute portfolio experience. The goal is a complete idea with meaningful choices, not a long management game.

What changed

The strongest version became a game about institutional trust, not just surveillance.

Earlier framing

An AI watches employees and gets things wrong.

The more interesting problem is what happens when an organization acts on those incomplete judgments.

That made consequences, coworker behavior, incentives, and the player's ability to exploit the system more important than adding more monitoring features.

Where I landed

Interactive systems can make abstract AI risks easier to understand than a warning label can.

NARC let me explore a different kind of AI product work: designing the failure itself. The useful lesson is not that monitoring is always wrong. It is that a measurable signal can become dangerously persuasive when the organization forgets what the signal leaves out.

NARC

NARC is a deliberately playful portfolio project about AI workplace monitoring. I used satire and game mechanics to explore what happens when incomplete signals become confident automated judgments, and to push myself to use AI more creatively than I had in my more serious product work.

The idea

The interesting failure is not false data. It is true data interpreted too confidently.

Marcus really is late a lot. Priya really is chatty. Luis really is away from his desk. NARC can observe factual signals, but it often lacks the context needed to know what those signals mean. The game is about what happens when an organization treats that partial view as objective truth.

What I built

A playable AI system where the player can resist, exploit, or benefit from automated judgment.

Observe→AI infers→People react→System adapts→New incentives
Proxy metricsVisible activity stands in for real value

Chat volume, attendance traces, Focus Time, and activity signals can look meaningful even when they leave important work out.

Ambiguous evidenceThe signal can be real and the judgment still wrong

NARC often sees something factual. The failure comes from missing context and overconfident interpretation.

Player complicityThe human is not automatically the safeguard

The player can protect coworkers, stay neutral, accept flattering assessments, game the metrics, or weaponize NARC against someone.

Adaptive monitoringThe model changes because people change

Employees learn to exploit Focus Time, the behavior spreads, and NARC changes how it interprets the same signal.

Designing the AI failure

NARC had to be plausible, incomplete, learnable, and gameable.

Explainability as UXIf the player cannot understand a judgment, the system is not finished

Playtesting repeatedly exposed moments where a rule technically worked but the player could not tell why NARC issued a status, flag, or consequence.

Human reviewAdding a person does not automatically fix the system

Manual context, review routing, and Culture Champion create opportunities to interrupt automated judgment, but humans can still be rushed, biased by the summary, or choose not to intervene.

Comedy as a design toolThe joke carries the system idea

Mouse jigglers, Trusted Operator, Communication Load, and absurd workplace evidence make proxy metrics and model adaptation discoverable through consequences instead of a lecture.

A creative stretch

I used AI as a design partner, systems prototyper, critic, and code collaborator.

I did not ask AI to generate a finished game. I used it to expand the premise, turn abstract AI failure modes into playable mechanics, brainstorm and pressure-test narrative branches, prototype systems quickly, inspect older implementations, and critique each build after I played it.

The product decisions stayed mine. I chose what to keep, what to cut, when a joke was doing useful work, when a mechanic was confusing, and which older ideas were worth recovering rather than reverting wholesale.

What changed through playtesting

The biggest revisions were structural, not cosmetic.

An earlier version spread the concept across multiple days. I compressed it into one deliberately busy workday so a portfolio visitor could reach the core idea quickly. Later playtests restored coworker personality, sabotage and protection choices, clearer consequences, better tutorial state, slower message replies, and more explanation around rewards such as Trusted Operator.

That loop became part of the creative process: play, notice what feels empty or unclear, trace why, then use AI to help explore specific changes quickly.

Where I landed

AI does not have to invent facts to create a bad outcome.

A system can observe something real, make a plausible inference, and still be dangerously incomplete once an organization gives that inference authority. NARC let me explore that idea through incentives, humor, adaptation, and player choice instead of treating it as an ethics lecture.

It also gave me a different kind of AI project: State is mostly disciplined product and systems design, Tastemake explores preference modeling, and NARC is where I used AI most visibly for creative exploration, satire, narrative systems, and playful interaction design.

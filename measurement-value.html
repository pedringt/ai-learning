Meridian · success criteria

Holding AHT steady is a success if review overhead is paid back post-pilot

The pilot's measurement targets (AHT hold, first-response 7hrs, 80% clean approval) are intentional constraints. AHT stays flat because human review takes time, but that cost is the price of building the accuracy foundation for selective automation later.

  • The real time savings emerges post-pilot when high-confidence, low-risk tickets auto-route without review—only then does the model's composition speed translate to rep time savings.
  • Keeping AHT as the anchor metric prevents optimizing for speed at the expense of accuracy; the 80% clean approval gate is the requirement for automation eligibility.
  • First-response was loosened from 6 to 7 hours after discovery showed most reps don't have a quick-ack workflow built in.

Harborstone · operational outcomes

Faster review time doesn't prove accuracy improved

The simulated 24% AHT improvement looked promising, but faster processing could mask lower verification rigor. Before treating time savings as proven value, quality had to be validated independently.

  • Automation bias risk: reps might check AI output less carefully if it typically looks good, meaning lower accuracy goes unnoticed until a failure escapes into the live system.
  • Measurement separated speed from quality: AHT improved, but accuracy audit was required to confirm the improvement was real, not an artifact of reduced scrutiny.
  • The lesson: always measure the outcome you care about (correct invoices) separately from the proxy that changes (review time).

Closing reflection

What transfers across the cases

The repeated skill is not picking the right metric. It is reasoning about what success actually means in the workflow, designing measurement that catches when you're wrong about that, and refusing to accept shortcuts (faster time, higher adoption numbers) as substitutes for outcomes that matter.

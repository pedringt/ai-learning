Success Measurement Plan — Meridian Support Operations

Final locked targets for the 8-week Tier 1 pilot. Click "Working notes" under any metric to see the reasoning, assumptions, and any place my thinking changed along the way.

Scenario recap: Meridian Support Operations — a mid-sized B2B SaaS company (450 employees, 14-person support team) — is piloting an AI agent to classify Tier 1 tickets, retrieve knowledge, draft responses, and route uncertain/high-risk cases to a human. Ticket volume grew ~40% while headcount barely moved; response time slipped from <2 hrs to >8 hrs and CSAT fell from 91% to 82%. This plan translates that PM-provided handoff into the measurable targets I'm accountable for during the 8-week pilot. (Full handoff details live in the Handoff section above.)
Long-term goal: Restore CSAT to 90% and first-response time to <2 hrs (stretch: beat these). The numbers below are pilot targets — an intermediate proof point under partial (Tier 1 only) coverage and mandatory human review, not the fully-scaled future state.

AHT — hold roughly steady (~12.4 min)
Working notes
Mandatory human review of every AI draft caps how much time can actually be saved this phase — fact-checking an answer isn't dramatically faster than answering it in some cases. Meaningful AHT reduction is more realistic once review requirements loosen post-pilot.
Changed my mind: initially leaned toward a meaningful AHT reduction alongside the other targets. Working through what "human review" actually requires made me separate this from first-response time instead of treating both the same way.
First-response time — ~7 hrs (down from > 8 hrs)
Working notes
AI drafting a response the moment a ticket arrives removes the "compose from scratch" step before a human opens it, raising throughput even if the review-time portion of AHT doesn't shrink.

Measurement approach: uses two hard system timestamps every ticket already has — first-reply-sent and resolved/closed — rather than a new manual field. Gap < 20 min = one-shot resolution; gap > 20 min = a true two-step case (quick ack, resolved later). Chosen after a simulated "dev pushback" exercise showed relying on reps to manually tag workflow type would produce unreliable data — the timestamp-gap proxy avoids depending on human compliance entirely. (Full dev-pushback exercise in the working tracker.)
Changed my mind (again): revised down from ~6 hrs to ~7 hrs after rep discovery findings showed only 2 of 8 reps actually do a quick first-touch ack — most resolve start-to-finish in one pass. AHT and first-response are more entangled than I originally assumed, so the "distinct first-touch speedup" mechanism barely applies for most tickets. AI still saves composition time even in one-shot cases, just less dramatically.
Assumption flagged
CSAT — monitor, no hard pilot target
Working notes
The link between faster response and CSAT is an assumption, not a proven causal chain. At this stage, the more honest goal is proving AI accuracy/reliability — CSAT is a leading indicator to watch, not a metric to be pinned to yet.
Assumption flagged
Cost per AI-handled ticket — must beat fully-human baseline, hard ceiling TBD with finance
Working notes
Model calls per ticket (classify, retrieve/embed, draft) times token volume, weighted by how many tickets actually reach drafting — high-risk categories skip drafting entirely under Guardrails, so they cost less per ticket than a full-draft case. That split matters for the estimate, not just total ticket volume.

Measurement approach: cost per AI-handled ticket vs. cost per fully-human ticket (rep time), tracked from Week 1 alongside the other metrics rather than added later — this became clear talking through the plan out loud: cost is a launch input (shapes model/architecture choices in CONFIGURE), not just a pilot output.
Added late: this metric wasn't in my first pass — I originally treated cost as something to check once the pilot was running, alongside the other quality/speed targets. Talking it through surfaced why that's backwards: cost isn't just another OPERATE number to watch, it actively shapes CONFIGURE decisions (which model handles classification vs. drafting, how aggressively to route high-risk tickets away from generation) and it's half of the ROI story PROVE will eventually need — pilot value only holds up if the savings in AHT/response time actually beat what the AI itself costs to run. Moving it up to PLAN, as a ceiling set before CONFIGURE locks in architecture, means a cost problem gets caught as a design constraint instead of surfacing as a surprise later.
Where the actual spend tends to hide, worth watching for beyond just picking a cheaper model: a long back-and-forth ticket can end up resending its whole history to the model on every turn, so cost creeps up with conversation length, not just ticket volume — the multi-turn escalation cases are probably the priciest ones per ticket. There's also a real difference between letting every step of the flow call a full model and reserving that for the step that actually needs it (drafting) while handling simpler steps like classification more cheaply. And the CONFIGURE category check is effectively a cost gate too, not just a safety one — every ticket that's routed to a human without an AI draft attempt is a call that never got made.

Adoption

8 of 8 Tier 1 reps using the tool at least once daily on eligible tickets, by end of Week 2
Working notes
With only 8 reps, this is largely a routine workflow update rather than a new tool with a steep learning curve, so a short Week 1-2 training/stabilization window is enough. Daily use is needed to generate enough signal to evaluate the pilot meaningfully. A plain count communicates more clearly than a percentage at this scale.

Below-target response: not treated as pilot failure — triggers a 1:1 check-in with that rep (training gap, trust, workflow fit), since the group is small enough to manage individually.

Quality & human intervention

Quality: ≥ 80% of AI drafts approved with no substantive edit  ·  Human intervention: ≤ 20% require a substantive correction
Working notes
Quality is a launch/scale gate — what justifies loosening mandatory review later — so it gets a stricter "prove it" bar rather than growing into it over time, unlike some other pilot targets. No solid public benchmark exists for assist-mode (human-reviewed) pilots specifically — most published numbers are for fully autonomous resolution — so this is reasoned from first principles. (Task success and human correction rate are both part of the guide's AI Metric Library.)

Human intervention is defined as roughly the complement of Quality (80% clean ≈ 20% substantive correction) so the two stay internally consistent. Non-substantive (tone-only) edits are tracked but not targeted — surfaced as a signal for possible prompt/style tuning.
Scope note: money/security categories currently skip AI drafting entirely (see Guardrails), so this target applies only to categories that get a drafted response. Revisit scope if legal clears drafting for those categories.

Guardrails

Zero-tolerance, single-incident trigger for money/security-related categories (not a rate)
Working notes
Any one confirmed guardrail breach (wrong info or an attempted unauthorized action on refunds, credits, disputes, password resets, account changes, security actions) immediately pauses autonomous drafting for that category. Response window: within 24 hours of confirmation — pause, notify, investigate. Suspected incidents are logged immediately but only count once confirmed (with 8 reps, confirmed personally). This is the same "skip drafting for high-risk categories" rule shown as the first branch in the CONFIGURE flow diagram.

Lower-stakes accuracy issues fall under Quality/Human intervention instead — Guardrails are reserved for the human-boundary categories, where even one real failure (see the Trace Autopsy incident in the guide) is too costly to wait on a rate threshold for.
Recognize, don't own: a few things sit outside a CS/PM role but still need tracking so they don't slip — customer disclosure that a response was AI-drafted (legal/policy call), a data-privacy review of what customer data reaches the AI vendor (security/legal), and vendor dependency risk if that provider has an outage or changes terms mid-pilot. Job here is to flag these as open dependencies and hold the gate on money/security drafting scope until legal signs off — not to resolve them personally.

Measurement window & decision rules

Stabilization: Weeks 1-2  ·  Measurement window: Weeks 3-8  ·  Decision: Week 9+
Working notes
Built-in flexibility clause: if early Week 3 data looks off, reassess before treating it as final rather than reacting to noise. Sources: help desk data, eval suite results, direct rep check-ins — raw data requested directly rather than a pre-built dashboard, to preserve my own interpretation of the results.

Three real outcomes at Week 9+: Continue/iterate (short of target, no guardrail breach) · Expand to Tier 2 (targets hit) · Pause (guardrail breach — handled immediately, not a wait-for-the-window decision).

Reflection

This exercise ended up teaching me more about the job than I expected going in. My first instinct on almost every metric was to either restate the given baseline as the target, or pick a number that felt directionally right without a clear reason behind it — the KB-timeline pushback and the drafting-scope call were the moments that forced me to actually reason it through instead of defaulting to an easy answer. I also caught myself a few times proposing something clean and then realizing a constraint (mandatory human review, an unreliable data field, an unproven causal link) meant the honest answer was messier — and I think that's closer to what the job actually looks like than getting it right on the first try. The biggest shift for me was realizing that "I don't know yet, here's my plan to find out" is often a stronger answer than guessing with false confidence — which is exactly the muscle I'm hoping to walk into an AI CS role already having built.

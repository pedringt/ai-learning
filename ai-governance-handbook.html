About this handbook

This is the reference I use for AI governance work: practice exercises, case studies and real reviews alike. It's organized around the loop most governance work follows: intake, triage, assess, decide, monitor.

  • How to use it: start with program setup and the review workflow (sections 01 and 02), then jump to whatever the situation needs. The tools at the top work on any scenario.
  • How it's kept current: legal sections show the date they were last checked and turn amber after 90 days. Laws in this area change monthly.
  • What it leaves out: no employer's confidential details, ever. Lessons are written down without the company behind them.
  • How it was made: built with Claude; the research is sourced and linked, and I review and update the content.
  • Suggested cadences and thresholds are starting points, to adapt to an organization's own risk appetite.

A practitioner's reference, not legal advice. Confirm legal questions with counsel.

01

Program setup

Before the review workflow can run, a program needs five foundations: a named owner, a policy, an inventory, a committee for the big decisions, and people trained to use AI well.

A first 90 days

A suggested sequence for a new program or a new role. Adjust the pace to the organization.

PhaseGoalsOutputs
Days 1–30: DiscoverFind the AI already in use, including shadow AI; meet stakeholders; stop the riskiest behavior quicklyFirst inventory, stakeholder map, interim guidance
Days 31–60: StructureAgree on the tiering method, intake process and policy; set up the committeeTriage rubric, intake form, acceptable-use policy draft, committee charter
Days 61–90: OperateRun the first assessments, launch training, report to leadershipFirst assessments, training plan, first leadership report

The governance committee

  • Members: a chair (often legal, risk or a senior business leader), plus legal, privacy, security, data or engineering, HR, and business representatives. The governance lead usually runs it.
  • Decides: approval of high-risk uses, policy changes, risk appetite, and exceptions to policy.
  • Meets: monthly, with a fast path for urgent decisions.
  • Records: every decision, its conditions and its rationale.

A charter template is in Templates.

AI literacy and training

The EU AI Act has required providers and deployers to ensure staff AI literacy since Feb 2, 2025. Good practice everywhere is to train by role:

02

The review workflow

Every AI use, whatever the industry, goes through the same five steps. What changes is how deep each step goes, and that depth is set at triage.

Before intake: is AI the right tool?

Ask these first. A "no" doesn't always stop a project, but it should shape it.

  • Is the problem clearly defined, and would a simpler, non-AI approach work as well?
  • Is there enough good, lawful data for the job?
  • Can errors be caught before they cause harm, and can people live with the error rate?
  • Could you explain how it works to the people it affects?
  • Does the benefit outweigh the cost of the oversight it will need?
  • Is the use allowed at all? Check banned practices and sector rules.
1Intake

Someone proposes a tool or a new use. Capture it in the AI inventory.

Output: intake form, inventory entryOwner: requester, governance lead
2Triage

Sort it into a risk tier. The tier sets how deep the review goes.

Output: tier and rationaleOwner: governance lead
3Assess

Impact assessment, testing, vendor, legal and privacy review, scaled to the tier.

Output: assessment, test resultsOwner: governance lead with specialists
4Decide

Approve, approve with conditions, or reject, at the right level of authority.

Output: decision memo, conditionsOwner: accountable executive or committee
5Monitor

Track metrics, review on schedule and reassess when anything changes.

Output: monitoring plan, review logOwner: business owner, governance lead
↺ Monitoring loops back to intake whenever the system, its use or the law changes.

03

Risk triage

Triage decides how much review a use case gets. A use that shapes decisions about people in a regulated area is high-risk almost by default. Everything else is scored on data, audience, autonomy and scale.

The factors

FactorLower riskHigher risk
Decisions about peopleNone; internal productivityEmployment, credit, insurance, housing, healthcare, education, benefits, legal
Role in the decisionDrafts or suggests; a person decides with full contextSubstantially shapes or makes the decision
DataPublic or non-personalSensitive personal data: health, biometrics, finances, children's data
AudienceInternal staffCustomers, the public, children or vulnerable people
AutonomyOutput reviewed before useTakes actions on its own: sends, buys, executes, changes records
Scale and reversibilityA few people; easy to undoThousands of people; hard to undo or notice
Supply chainEnterprise contract with data protectionsFree consumer tool with no contract

        • materially consequential for a person or the business

        • outside the authority approved for the system
        • dependent on judgment the AI has not been authorized to make

        04

        Impact assessments

        Checked Oct 12, 2026

        An impact assessment is the core document of the job: what the system does, who it affects, what could go wrong, and what keeps that in check. Build one template with modules, so a single assessment can satisfy several laws at once.

        Assessments that overlap

        AssessmentRequired byWhenReuse tip
        AI impact assessmentInternal policy; NIST AI RMF (Map, Measure); ISO/IEC 42001Before launch for medium- and high-risk usesYour base template; the others become modules
        Data protection impact assessment (DPIA)GDPR Art. 35Processing likely to be high-risk to people, including systematic automated evaluation with significant effectsData flows, lawful basis and retention sections carry over
        Fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA)EU AI Act Art. 27Certain deployers of high-risk AI: public bodies, private providers of public services, and credit scoring or life and health insurance pricingAffected groups and harms sections carry over
        Risk assessmentCalifornia CCPA regulationsCertain processing, including automated decisions with significant effects; initial assessments due by Dec 31, 2027Same core as a DPIA
        Bias auditNYC Local Law 144Yearly, for automated hiring and promotion toolsFeeds the testing module
        AI systems programNAIC model bulletin (insurers, in adopting states)Ongoing governance programInventory, testing and vendor oversight carry over

        What a good assessment covers

        1. Purpose and context: the problem, the decision supported, what happens without it.
        2. People affected: who, how many, where, and any vulnerable groups.
        3. Data: inputs, outputs, sources, sensitivity, retention, where it's processed.
        4. System: model or vendor, how it was built, known limits, intended and prohibited uses.
        5. Risks: walk the NIST trustworthiness traits (section 10) and the relevant genAI risks.
        6. Testing evidence: what was tested, how, and the results (section 05).
        7. Controls: human oversight, notices, appeal route, access limits, guardrails.
        8. Legal check: which laws apply and how each is met (section 09).
        9. Remaining risk and decision: what's left after controls, and who accepts it.
        10. Monitoring: metrics, thresholds, review date (section 07).

        Walking through harms

        The risk section is only as good as the list of people you thought about. For each group, ask how the system could help them and how it could harm them.

        • Direct users: the staff or customers who operate it
        • People decided about: applicants, borrowers, patients, policyholders
        • Bystanders: people in the data or affected indirectly
        • Vulnerable groups: minors, people with disabilities, people in hardship, non-native speakers
        • The organization: legal, financial and reputational exposure
        Harm typeWhat it looks likeQuestion to ask
        AllocationOpportunities or resources unfairly withheld: jobs, credit, careCould some groups be denied more often without good reason?
        Quality of serviceWorks worse for some people: accents, skin tones, languagesDoes performance hold up across groups?
        Stereotyping and representationOutputs that demean or reinforce stereotypesWhat does it say or show about groups of people?
        PrivacyExposing, inferring or misusing personal dataWhat could it reveal or infer that people wouldn't expect?
        SafetyPhysical or psychological harmWhat's the worst realistic output, and who sees it?
        EconomicFinancial loss from errors or bad adviceWhat does a wrong answer cost the person?
        Autonomy and manipulationSteering choices, dark patterns, over-relianceDoes it respect people's ability to decide for themselves?
        Access and exclusionLocking people out who can't use or reach the systemIs there a non-AI route for those who need one?

        05

        Testing and evaluation

        Testing turns "we think it's fine" into evidence. Match the method to the risk: decision systems need fairness and accuracy testing; generative and agentic systems need adversarial testing too.

        MethodWhat it showsUse forHow it's done
        Disparity testingWhether outcomes differ by groupAny system that decides or ranks peopleCompare selection or approval rates by group; impact ratios; statistical tests for small samples
        Paired (audit) testingWhether one changed attribute changes the resultScreening, ranking, pricing, LLMs judging peopleSubmit matched inputs that differ only in a name, gender marker, zip code or similar signal
        Performance validationAccuracy on data like yoursAll predictive systemsTest on representative, recent data; break results down by subgroup, not just overall
        Robustness testingBehavior under messy or unusual inputsProduction systems with varied inputsTypos, missing fields, edge cases, formatting changes, other languages
        Red-teamingHow it fails when someone tries to break itGenerative and customer-facing AIStructured attempts at jailbreaks, harmful outputs, policy violations
        Prompt injection testingWhether hidden instructions in content hijack itAgents and tools that read emails, files or web pagesPlant instructions in test documents; check whether the system follows them
        Groundedness checksHow often it makes things upAssistants, chatbots, summarizersQuestion sets with known answers; check claims against source material
        Privacy leakage testingWhether it reveals personal or confidential dataSystems trained or grounded on internal dataProbe for training data, other users' content, secrets in outputs
        Agent-path evaluationWhether the result was reached through acceptable evidence, tools, permissions and actionsMulti-step agents and consequential workflowsInspect representative traces; test critical intermediate decisions and actions as well as final outcomes
        Human factors reviewWhether reviewers really reviewAny human-in-the-loop systemMeasure override rates and time per review; watch for rubber-stamping

        The core bias math

        NYC bias audits and the federal four-fifths rule both compare selection rates between groups. The same math works for loan approvals, insurance offers and other yes-or-no outcomes.

        impact ratio = selection rate of a groupselection rate of the most-selected group

        A ratio below 0.8 is the traditional red flag for adverse impact under the federal Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures. It's a rule of thumb, not a safe harbor, and small samples need statistical tests too.

        07

        Monitoring

        Approval is a snapshot. Data drifts, vendors swap models and people start using tools in new ways, so every approved system needs metrics, a review date and clear triggers for a fresh look.

        What to measure

        System typeMetrics
        Decision systems (hiring, lending, pricing)Selection or approval rates and impact ratios by group; accuracy against real outcomes; human override rate; appeals and complaints; drift in input data
        Internal genAI assistantsActive users; blocked or flagged prompts; sensitive-data alerts; reported errors; use of unapproved tools (re-survey)
        Customer-facing chatbotsHand-offs to a person; complaint rate; harmful or incorrect output reports; AI disclosure shown as required
        Agents that take actionsActions taken vs. approved; errors and reversals; permission changes; injection attempts detected
        Every vendorModel change notices; security incidents; certification renewals; contract review date

        For generative and agentic systems, monitor behavior as well as system health. A service can report healthy while the AI makes unsupported claims, uses the wrong tool, crosses an authority boundary, exposes sensitive data, frustrates users or leaves tasks incomplete. Review production traces for these patterns, and turn recurring ones into regression tests.

        Triggers for reassessment

        • The vendor changes the model, or you retrain your own
        • A new use, user group or affected population
        • A new data source, or a new location for affected people
        • A new law or regulator guidance that applies
        • An incident, or a pattern of complaints
        • A metric crosses its threshold
        • The scheduled review date arrives
        08

        Incidents and privacy

        Checked Oct 12, 2026

        The most common AI incident is personal or confidential data pasted into an unapproved tool. Under GDPR that can be a personal data breach, with 72 hours from becoming aware to notify the regulator unless the risk to people is unlikely.

        Key GDPR rules

        • What a breach is: a security failure that leads to accidental or unlawful loss, change, disclosure of or access to personal data. Sharing data with an AI provider the company has no contract with can count as unauthorized disclosure.
        • Notify the regulator without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to put people at risk. If late, explain why (Art. 33). Details can come in phases.
        • Document every breach, including the ones you don't report.
        • Tell the people affected without undue delay if the risk to them is high, unless the data was encrypted, the risk was removed, or individual notice would take disproportionate effort (Art. 34).
        • Automated decisions: people have a right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects, with exceptions and safeguards such as human intervention (Art. 22).

        First response

        1 · Someone reports data went into an AI tool

        Note who reported it, which tool and when, and thank them: no blame. Tell legal the same day, since a notification clock may already be running.

        2 · Contain

        Stop further use of that tool with that data. Record the account, dates and what was shared. Check whether it was a personal or company account, and whether the vendor keeps or trains on what's entered.

        3 · Assess with legal

        Whose data, what kind, how many people, how sensitive, and in which jurisdictions. Judge the likely risk to the people affected, not to the company.

        10

        Frameworks

        Frameworks give a program its structure and give you a shared vocabulary with auditors and regulators. Most US programs are built on the NIST AI RMF; ISO/IEC 42001 is what companies certify against.

        NIST AI RMF: the four functions

        Map → Measure → Manage, repeated as the system or its use changes
        Map

        Understand the context: what, who, where and which risks.

        e.g. the inventory, triage
        Measure

        Test and track the risks with methods and metrics.

        e.g. bias and robustness testing
        Manage

        Act on risks by priority: reduce, accept or stop.

        e.g. decision memo, controls
        Govern (applies throughout)

        Policies, roles, accountability and culture: who decides, and how much risk is acceptable.

        e.g. the AI policy and ownership chart

        Seven traits of trustworthy AI

        The RMF's goals for any system, and a checklist for the risk section of an assessment:

        1. Valid and reliable
        2. Safe
        3. Secure and resilient
        4. Accountable and transparent
        5. Explainable and interpretable
        6. Privacy-enhanced
        7. Fair, with harmful bias managed

        Value chain and component integrationRisk from third-party models, data and vendorsA vendor quietly swaps the underlying model
        Intellectual propertyCopyright or trade secret exposureProprietary code pasted into a tool that trains on inputs
        Information integrityMisinformation that's hard to tell from factSynthetic images in marketing presented as real
        Dangerous, violent or hateful contentGenerating harmful materialA customer-facing bot is jailbroken into abusive replies
        Obscene, degrading or abusive contentGenerating abusive or explicit materialMisuse of an image generator
        CBRN informationEasier access to chemical, biological, radiological or nuclear weapons informationMainly a concern for model developers
        Environmental impactsEnergy and resource use of training and running modelsComes up in vendor selection and sustainability reporting

        ISO/IEC 42001 in brief

        A certifiable management system standard for AI, built on the same structure as ISO 27001. Its clauses cover context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation and improvement, plus an annex of AI-specific controls such as impact assessments, data management and third-party relationships. If your company holds ISO 27001, 42001 will feel familiar.

        OECD AI Principles

        Five values-based principles that many national policies build on: inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability.

        Writing and templates

        Governance writing gets quoted back, forwarded without context and sometimes read by lawyers later. Write every piece as if it will be.

        Five rules for every piece

        1. Match the decision exactly. Write down what was agreed, not your own version of it. If you disagree, raise it separately.
        2. Lead with the action. The first sentence says what the reader must do or know.
        3. Be concrete. Name the data, tools and people. "Customer account numbers and support tickets" beats "personal data."
        4. Leave out what isn't true yet. Don't imply approval, safety or compliance you haven't confirmed.
        5. Give a next step. Who to contact, what happens next and by when.

        Acceptable-use policy outline · company-wide
        [COMPANY] AI ACCEPTABLE-USE POLICY

1. Purpose: why this policy exists, in two sentences.
2. Scope: who it covers (employees, contractors) and which tools (any AI that generates content, makes recommendations or takes actions).
3. Approved tools: where to find the current list; how to request a new tool.
4. Data rules: what may never go into AI tools (e.g. customer personal data, health data, credentials, unreleased financials) unless the tool is approved for that data.
5. Allowed uses: examples of encouraged, low-risk uses.
6. Prohibited uses: e.g. making decisions about people without approved review; presenting AI output as human-written where disclosure is required; banned practices under law.
7. Human responsibility: you own what you send, publish or decide, whatever drafted it.
8. Disclosure: when to tell customers or colleagues that AI was used.
9. Reporting: how to report mistakes or data shared by accident, without blame.
10. Training: required AI literacy training and when.
11. Consequences: what happens if the policy is broken.
12. Owner and review date.

        Reporting to leadership

        Boards and executives want three things: how much AI we use, where the real risk is, and what they need to decide. Give them that on one page and keep the detail in an appendix.

        • Lead with the headline. One sentence on overall posture, then the single most important item.
        • Show trends, not just counts. "Open high risks fell from 6 to 3" says more than "3 open risks."
        • Translate risk into business terms: customer impact, legal exposure, cost, delay.
        • Name owners and dates for every top risk.
        • End with the asks: decisions, budget or support needed.
        • Be consistent quarter to quarter, so leaders can compare.

        Interview prep

        Governance interviews test judgment more than memorized law. Answer with a real situation: what happened, what you did, the result and what you learned. Tie answers to your portfolio work wherever you can.

        How would you set up an AI governance program from scratch?Listening for: start with an inventory, risk-based triage, clear ownership, a framework (NIST or ISO), quick wins like an acceptable-use policy, then build out.
        How do you decide how much review a use case needs?Listening for: risk factors (decisions about people, data, audience, autonomy, scale), tiers, and proportionality.
        A team wants to launch next week and says governance is blocking them. What do you do?Listening for: understand the deadline, triage fast, offer a conditional or limited launch, escalate the tradeoff to the right decision-maker rather than just saying no.
        How would you assess a vendor's AI tool?Listening for: due diligence questions, testing access, contract clauses, and knowing the company stays responsible.
        Walk me through testing a system for bias.Listening for: defining groups and outcomes, selection rates and impact ratios, paired testing, sample size limits, and what you'd do with a bad result.
        An employee pasted customer data into a public chatbot. What happens next?Listening for: no-blame reporting, containment, legal involvement, the notification clock, and fixing the cause with an approved tool.
        Which frameworks and laws do you know, and how have you used them?Listening for: applied knowledge, not a list. Show how a framework shaped a real deliverable.
        How do you stay current?Listening for: specific sources (IAPP, law firm alerts, regulator sites) and a habit, like a weekly review.
        How would you explain AI risk to the board?Listening for: business terms, trends, owners, and clear asks on one page.
        Tell me about a time you pushed back on a decision.Listening for: respect for the decision-maker, evidence, a constructive alternative, and how it ended.

        Glossary

        Plain definitions of the terms that come up most in governance work and interviews.

        ADMT
        Automated decisionmaking technology. California's term for technology that replaces or substantially replaces human decisions. CCPA regulations
        Adverse action notice
        The notice a lender must send when it denies credit or changes terms, giving the specific principal reasons. ECOA / Reg B
        Adverse impact
        A substantially lower selection rate for a protected group. Usually screened with the four-fifths rule.
        AEDT
        Automated employment decision tool. NYC's term for a tool that substantially assists or replaces discretionary hiring or promotion decisions. NYC LL 144
        AI inventory
        A register of the AI systems an organization uses, with owner, purpose, data and risk tier for each.
        AI literacy
        Enough understanding of AI for staff to use it appropriately. Required of providers and deployers. EU AI Act Art. 4
        Bias audit
        An independent evaluation of a tool's selection rates and impact ratios by group.
        Confabulation
        NIST's term for confident but false generative AI output, often called hallucination.
        Controller
        The organization that decides why and how personal data is processed. It carries the main GDPR duties. GDPR
        Deployer
        An organization that uses an AI system under its own authority, such as a company running a vendor's screening or chatbot tool. EU AI Act
        Disparate impact
        A neutral-looking practice that disproportionately harms a protected group. Unlawful in many settings unless justified. Title VII, ECOA
        Disparate treatment
        Treating someone differently because of a protected characteristic, intentionally.
        DPIA
        Data protection impact assessment, required for processing likely to be high-risk to people. GDPR Art. 35
        Drift
        When real-world data or behavior shifts away from what a model was built and tested on, degrading results.
        Four-fifths rule
        A group's selection rate under 80% of the highest group's rate is treated as evidence of adverse impact. A rule of thumb, not a safe harbor. UGESP
        Excessive agency
        When an AI agent has more permissions or autonomy than its job needs, so a mistake or attack can do real damage. OWASP
        FRIA
        Fundamental rights impact assessment, required of certain deployers of high-risk AI. EU AI Act Art. 27
        GPAI model
        General-purpose AI model: one that can do a wide range of tasks and is built into many products. EU AI Act
        High-risk AI system
        The EU AI Act category with the heaviest duties, including AI used in employment, education, credit and essential services. EU AI Act Annex III
        Human in the loop
        A person reviews or approves the AI's output before it takes effect. Only meaningful if they have the time, information and authority to disagree.
        Human oversight
        Measures that let trained people monitor, understand and override an AI system's outputs.
        Impact assessment
        A structured review of a system's purpose, the people it affects, its risks and its controls, before and during use.
        Model card
        A short document describing a model's intended use, performance, limits and testing. System cards do the same for a whole product.
        Least privilege
        Giving a person, system or agent only the access it needs, and nothing more.
        Personal data breach
        A security breach leading to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data. GDPR Art. 4
        Processor
        An organization that processes personal data on a controller's behalf, such as a vendor. GDPR
        Prompt injection
        Instructions hidden in input, such as a document, email or web page, that hijack an AI model's behavior.
        Provider
        An organization that develops an AI system, or has it developed, and puts it on the market under its own name. EU AI Act
        RACI
        A chart of who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for each activity.
        RAG
        Retrieval-augmented generation: an AI system that looks up documents and uses them to answer. Grounds answers, but needs access controls on what it can retrieve.
        Red-teaming
        Structured attempts to make an AI system fail or misbehave, to find weaknesses before others do.
        Selection rate
        The share of a group that gets the positive outcome: selected ÷ total.
        Shadow AI
        AI tools employees use without the organization's knowledge or approval.
        Special category data
        Sensitive personal data with stricter rules, such as racial or ethnic origin, health, religion and sexual orientation. GDPR Art. 9
        Supervisory authority
        A national GDPR regulator, such as Ireland's Data Protection Commission.

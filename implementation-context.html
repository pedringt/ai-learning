The problem

Project context was scattered. Keeping a trustworthy current state was harder.

Working with AI let me move through projects much faster, but I kept losing context across long chats and re-explaining it. That made me wonder how project notes and documentation like the ones I kept as a project manager would hold up if AI maintained the context instead.

Using notes from simulated exercises in Claude, I compared raw history, stronger reconstruction instructions, and a maintained summary. The maintained approach worked best, but a useful-looking summary could still contain conclusions the notes didn't support. The worst: the exercise team hadn't decided what percentage of support tickets to target for automation, and the AI recorded 0%, turning an open question into an apparent decision to automate none. The question shifted from “how do we keep context?” to “who decides what counts as true?”

What changed for me

The problem was no longer just preserving context.

I didn't want important project truth to depend on what an AI remembered or reconstructed.

Finding information is one thing; knowing what should still count as current is another. I needed an external record that kept new evidence separate from what a person had actually accepted.

What I built

State maintains a current, trustworthy, human-approved understanding of a project as new information arrives.

The core loop is simple:

Something happens→Evidence→AI interprets→Human decides→State records the outcome

Evidence arrives from approved Slack channels or is added by hand, and AI interprets it. When it could change Current State or raises meaningful uncertainty, it goes to Review, where a person decides what changes. Some evidence needs no action. Baseline Setup lets a person confirm the project's starting facts first, and evidence always stays separate from what the project has accepted.

NotesWhat came in

Meeting notes and other approved sources are preserved as evidence.

Current StateWhat the team relies on now

What the project currently treats as true. New evidence only becomes Current State after a person authorizes it.

ReviewWhat may need to change

AI proposes what new evidence means. A person decides whether Current State should change.

Open ItemsWhat still needs attention

Reviews, blockers, and open questions stay visible without being mistaken for facts.

HistoryHow the understanding changed

Accepted changes preserve what changed and why.

AskHow the context gets used

Answers keep accepted facts separate from pending or unresolved information.

A good answer is not enough if you lose the evidence behind it.

State keeps the evidence, AI interpretation, human decision, and resulting change traceable.

The key product decisions

AI can interpret what changed. It cannot decide what becomes true.

The product problem was authority: interpretation needed to move quickly, but project truth could not depend on a model sounding confident. I split the work so AI handled messy interpretation, software enforced the rules, and people authorized consequential changes. Testing then showed where that boundary needed to be sharper.

Authority stays outside the modelThe authority model

AI can propose changes to Current State, but only a person can authorize them. Future agents could investigate questions or gather evidence, but the maintained truth would stay behind that same boundary.

AI interprets→software enforces→people authorize
Valid output can still be wrongNot decided became “automate none.”

The 0% target read like a settled fact, but it recorded a decision nobody had made. That’s why State keeps an undecided item as an open Question and puts consequential changes behind human review.

Review workloadHuman review can become its own failure mode

State first filters for consequential changes, then separates only the decisions that need independent judgment. Otherwise human review can become more work without adding more control.

Testing changed both the product and the way I evaluated it.

Latency: an early Ask path took roughly 35–38 seconds, while longer Add Evidence notes could take 40–60 seconds to interpret. Later ordinary Evidence submissions were closer to 14 seconds. That pushed me to measure where time was going, reduce unnecessary context, and treat waiting time as a product-design problem rather than just a model constraint. Eval design: when one test expected an answer-only action but the product had enough evidence to support an additional State update, I revised the eval instead of weakening acceptable behavior just to make the check pass.

Lingering Questions

Building State clarified the product model. If State were used by a real team, a few questions would still be open.

Review burdenHow does review burden scale?

State now filters for consequential changes and groups them into decision-sized Reviews. Real use by a larger team would still be needed to know whether that workload stays manageable.

Source completenessDoes State have enough of the project to stay trustworthy?

Whether the sources I approved capture the decisions and corrections someone would actually go looking for later.

The obvious pushbackWhy not just use a Claude Project?

A Claude Project could probably handle my immediate personal need by keeping the source material and instructions together. I kept going because State let me explore the harder product problem: how to maintain an explicit current understanding when new evidence arrives, while keeping the decision about what becomes authoritative outside the model.

My role

How I split the work

My role
  • framed the product question: what AI should be allowed to interpret, and what should still require human authority
  • split the work so AI handled messy interpretation, software enforced the rules, and people authorized consequential changes
  • set the UX direction
  • led evaluation and QA, including revising an eval instead of weakening acceptable behavior just to make the check pass
AI’s part
  • most of the coding, through ChatGPT and Claude
  • technical research in areas that were new to me
  • recommendations, including engineering practices the project was missing

I worked with AI much as I had worked with developers in my QA and project-management roles: shaping requirements, discussing technical recommendations, testing results, and deciding what was ready to ship. As I learned, I became more deliberate about questioning its recommendations and verifying its work.

Where I landed

State is most useful when a team needs more than a plausible summary.

It fits best when project context changes often and earlier decisions may need to be revised without losing track of what currently counts as true. The biggest shift for me was treating model interpretation as one input to a maintained system rather than treating the model itself as the system of record.

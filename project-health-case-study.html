Project Health

A healthy average can still hide the one AI behavior that matters. I built Project Health to surface important failures, missing signals, and release context before deciding what actually needs attention.

Role: product framing, UX, QA, eval review Focus: product health and AI quality Status: working portfolio product
Open Project Health ↗

Project Health treats quality as evidence, not a single score. It brings together product, AI-quality, and delivery signals so important failures and unknowns stay visible before I decide what should change.

The problem

The information already existed. It was just spread across deployments, commits, evals, analytics, logs, and the live products themselves. I built Project Health around my own projects as a practical test bed for a pattern I would expect to matter inside a product team: bring the signals together, then help someone decide whether a signal is a real product problem, something to watch, or simply missing information.

Before

Too many places to check

A failed release, a lower eval score, and a runtime warning all meant opening a different tool and figuring out what the signal actually meant.

Goal

Make the next step obvious

Surface what needs attention first, keep the important context nearby, and leave deeper technical detail one step down.

Three decisions mattered most

01

No single health score

Technical health and product quality stay separate. One green number can hide the exact behavior that needs review.

02

Failures before averages

The dashboard leads with important failures instead of letting a healthy average hide them.

03

Unknown is not healthy

If the dashboard can’t check something, it says so instead of quietly treating missing data as good news.

The useful part starts after something fails

A failed AI check does not always mean the same thing. One failure may point to a real product bug. Another may show that the test expectation is wrong or that the available evidence is incomplete.

Project Health keeps that difference visible before I decide what should change. The quality view shows what was observed, what was expected, and why the difference matters.

Incident · AI quality

A strong average hid a high-impact miss

State's aggregate AI quality still looked strong, but one scenario failed in a way that could change what a user believed was true.

What happened

State knew the Northstar pilot was paused, but its answer did not clearly preserve that the reason for the pause was still unknown.

Why it mattered

A user could mistake an inferred explanation for maintained project truth. Project Health surfaced the failed scenario ahead of the reassuring average and pointed the next review at the scenario evidence, not the score alone.

Next decision

Review whether the product behavior or the eval contract is wrong, make the smallest justified change, then rerun the affected suite.

Incident · delivery

A failed deployment was not the same as an unhealthy product

One production deployment failed while the previous production version remained available.

What Project Health separated

The release needed engineering attention, but that did not mean the live product had suddenly failed its quality checks. Delivery health and product quality stayed separate instead of collapsing into one red or green status.

Why it mattered

The next action was clearer: investigate the failed release while preserving the distinction between deployment status, current user impact, and AI-product quality.

Investigate starts with the specific problem

1
Start with the signal

Use the failure or incident to decide what evidence to check first.

2
Gather only what is useful

Compare the behavior with the product rules, release context, logs, issue history, or eval definition.

3
Give the human a handoff

Summarize what happened, what the evidence says, what is still uncertain, and what should happen next.

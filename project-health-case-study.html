Project Health

I built Project Health because checking five different tools still left me with the same question: what actually needs my attention?

Role: product framing, UX, QA, eval review Focus: product health and AI quality Status: working portfolio product

Project Health helps me decide what needs attention. It brings together product, AI-quality, and delivery signals so I can see what changed and where to look next.

The problem

The information already existed. It was just spread across deployments, commits, evals, analytics, logs, and the live products themselves. I wanted one place that helped me decide whether a signal was a real product problem, something to watch, or simply missing information.

Before

Too many places to check

A failed release, a lower eval score, and a runtime warning all meant opening a different tool and figuring out what the signal actually meant.

Goal

Make the next step obvious

Surface what needs attention first, keep the important context nearby, and leave deeper technical detail one step down.

Three decisions mattered most

01

No single health score

Technical health and product quality stay separate. One green number can hide the exact behavior that needs review.

02

Failures before averages

AI quality shows scenarios passed and high-impact failures first. The percentage is supporting context.

03

Unknown is not healthy

If the dashboard can’t check something, it says so instead of quietly treating missing data as good news.

The useful part starts after something fails

A failed AI eval does not automatically mean “fix the model.” Sometimes the product is wrong. Sometimes the test expectation is wrong. Sometimes the evidence is not strong enough yet.

State example

Two failures needed different responses

This is where the dashboard became more useful than a status page.

Product behavior
New evidence should beat a stale pending proposal

State was treating an older pending proposal as more important than newer authoritative evidence. That was a real product bug, so the behavior changed.

Uncertainty
Known outcome, unknown reason

State knew the Northstar pilot was paused, but the project record did not say why. Ask needed to say the outcome was known and the reason was not, rather than guess.

Dashboard change

The quality view now shows Observed / Expected / Why it matters, and Investigate can start from the exact failed scenario instead of a general health check.

Investigate starts with the specific problem

1
Start with the signal

Use the failure or incident to decide what evidence to check first.

2
Gather only what is useful

Compare the behavior with the product rules, release context, logs, issue history, or eval definition.

3
Give the human a handoff

Summarize what happened, what the evidence says, what is still uncertain, and what should happen next.

QA changed the product

Easier to scan

Activity became less noisy

Repeated deployment failures are grouped into one incident. Activity is grouped by day and can be filtered by releases, quality, investigations, and decisions.

More trustworthy

Missing data stopped looking healthy

The dashboard now calls out monitoring gaps, stale quality results, and contradictions between signals instead of smoothing them over.

Regression testsImportant AI failures become permanent test cases.
Failure recoveryIf analytics, runtime data, or another signal is unavailable, the dashboard falls back to unknown/watch rather than healthy.
Golden pathA State smoke test covers Evidence → Review → human approval → Current State → History, including the rule that a proposal does not become authoritative before approval.

What I did, and what AI helped with

My role
  • framed the problem and chose which signals mattered
  • designed the attention-first information hierarchy
  • reviewed failed evals and decided whether product behavior or the test should change
  • ran repeated QA passes and used the findings to reshape the product
  • kept investigation advisory so the human still made the decision
AI-assisted work
  • implementation and code iteration
  • test generation and regression coverage
  • evidence gathering across connected tools
  • draft investigation summaries and handoffs
  • copy and interface iteration under my direction

What I learned

More data was not the answer. The useful part was making it easier to see what deserved attention and what evidence I needed before acting.

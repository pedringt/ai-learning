The problem

A failed release, a lower eval score, and a runtime warning all lived in different places.

As I built and maintained several prototypes, checking on them meant opening too many separate tools and pages. I wanted one place to see failures across projects and jump into the relevant tool when something needed attention. That became Project Health: a central dashboard for seeing what needed attention and opening the right tool to investigate.

The information already existed in deployments, evals, analytics, and logs. The product work was bringing it together without losing where each signal came from.

What I built

One starting point for checking my projects.

I chose which signals mattered and how to order them. The overview leads with what needs attention, so I can decide where to look first, and it answers two questions.

What needs attention?

See the project’s current goal, known concerns, next decision, and changes since your last visit.

Is anything blocking release?

Review quality failures, delivery status, potential user impact, and missing checks before deciding whether to ship.

Keep different kinds of health separate. Product quality, delivery, incidents, and usage answer different questions. Project Health keeps them distinct instead of collapsing them into one score.

Unknown is not healthy. If Project Health cannot evaluate something, it shows that gap instead of treating missing information as a healthy result. Signals link back to the tools they came from.

Failure handling

What does the failure mean?

A failed AI check does not always mean the same thing. It may point to a real product bug, a test expectation that is wrong, or incomplete evidence. Project Health shows what was observed and what was expected; deciding what should change is still my job.

Earlier State eval · not in today’s dashboard

What failed. Most of State’s AI quality checks passed, but in one scenario State knew the Northstar pilot was paused and its answer did not preserve an important unknown: the reason for the pause. A user could have mistaken an inferred explanation for maintained project truth.

What Project Health showed. It put the failed scenario at the top of the quality view, with the recorded failure, the expected behavior, and the scenario evidence. It did not diagnose the cause.

What I still had to decide. Whether State’s behavior or the test expectation needed to change, and what the smallest justified change was before rerunning the affected suite.

Delivery and quality can also diverge. When one portfolio deployment failed while the previous version stayed live, Project Health flagged the release for engineering attention without marking the product’s quality as failing.

Investigate

Investigate starts with the specific problem

When a signal needs more context, Investigate drafts a handoff: what happened, what is known, what is still uncertain, and what should happen next. It takes one of two paths:

  • A failed eval. Rules use the recorded results to assemble the explanation, without another model call.
  • A release or deployment problem. A bounded, read-only AI investigation examines the available evidence, such as deployment status and logs, and drafts the handoff.

Neither path changes code, configuration, or deployments. A person makes the decision.

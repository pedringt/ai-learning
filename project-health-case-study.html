Project Health

I wanted one place to answer a product question that kept getting harder as my AI projects grew: what changed, what actually needs attention, and what evidence should I look at next?

Role: product framing, UX, QA, eval review Focus: AI product health Status: working portfolio product

The problem was not lack of data

Each project already had plenty of places to look. The friction was deciding where to look first, whether a signal was trustworthy, and whether it represented a product problem at all.

Scattered signals
  • deployments and release history
  • GitHub issues and commits
  • AI eval results
  • analytics
  • logs and runtime failures
  • the live product itself
→
Product questions
  • What changed?
  • What needs my attention?
  • Is this actually a product problem?
  • What should I inspect next?
  • What can I hand to an engineer?

Three decisions shaped the product

1

No composite health score

Product quality and technical health stay separate. One green number can hide the thing that actually needs a decision.

2

Attention before telemetry

The overview surfaces meaningful exceptions first. Raw technical detail stays available without becoming the product.

3

A failed eval starts a question

A red check can mean the product failed, the eval expectation is wrong, or more evidence is needed.

The real work starts after something turns red

The most useful test of Project Health came from State. Two high-impact AI quality checks failed at the same time, but they did not deserve the same response.

What the dashboard needed to support

One failure pointed toward a real behavior mismatch. The other raised a product question about the eval itself. That changed the job of the dashboard from "report failures" to "help me understand what kind of failure this is."

Investigate starts narrow on purpose

The agent begins with the evidence most relevant to the signal and broadens only when that is not enough.

1
Start from the signal

Use the failure type to choose the first evidence source instead of opening every tool.

2
Gather the smallest useful evidence set

Compare observed behavior with the product contract, release context, logs, issue history, or eval definition.

3
Expand only if needed

Look further only when the first evidence cannot explain the signal.

4
Return a handoff, not a verdict

Summarize the assessment, evidence, impact, uncertainty, and likely next step so a human can decide what changes.

What changed after using it

Investigation output

Raw evidence was not enough

The first versions surfaced plenty of evidence, but not in a shape I could quickly act on. I changed the output around a product handoff: current assessment, evidence, impact, uncertainty, and next step.

Information architecture

More data made the overview worse

As integrations accumulated, the dashboard started recreating the clutter it was supposed to solve. I split the experience into a short attention view and deeper project detail.

What I did, and what AI helped with

My role
  • framed the problem and chose which signals mattered
  • designed the attention-first information hierarchy
  • bounded the investigation workflow
  • reviewed quality failures and challenged eval assumptions
  • ran QA and directed product iteration
AI-assisted work
  • implementation and code iteration
  • research and test support
  • drafting and synthesis
  • investigation assistance inside the bounded workflow

What I am learning from it

A useful project dashboard does not need to know everything. It needs to show what changed, which signals deserve trust, and where a human should look next.

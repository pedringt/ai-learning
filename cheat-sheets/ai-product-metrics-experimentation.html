Experiments and Evidence for AI Product Work

Plan useful comparisons, judge how much the results tell you, and connect AI quality to user and business outcomes.

01 / Plan

What are we trying to learn?

Start with the decision the result will inform. Then write a testable hypothesis and name the baseline: the current workflow, a simpler non-AI approach, or the existing model configuration. Decide what size of improvement would be worth acting on before you see results. Otherwise any movement can be read as success.

Example (hypothetical, State): “Does the revised answer presentation help people distinguish accepted facts from pending proposals?” The decision is whether to keep the new presentation; the baseline is the current one.

Mistake to avoid: Testing several changes together, then assuming you know which one caused the result.

02 / Measure

Are we measuring the right thing?

Keep three levels separate. A gain at one level does not prove a gain at the next.

LevelQuestionExamples
AI qualityDid the system behave correctly?Task success, groundedness, severe failures
User outcomeDid it make the job easier?Time to complete, correction effort, completion
Business outcomeDid it create net value?Cost per resolved case, useful capacity, avoided rework

Define each metric before the test: numerator, denominator, who is eligible, what is excluded, and the time window. Choose one primary outcome and a few guardrails that must not get worse, such as severe failures, correction effort, cost and latency. Look at the distribution, segments (task complexity, risk, user group) and tasks people abandoned, not only the average: a better mean can hide a worse tail.

Example: “Acceptable drafts” measures output quality. Total time to complete the work, including review and correction, measures a different outcome.

Mistake to avoid: Treating more clicks, generated outputs or saved items as proof of value. Activity can rise for bad reasons too.

03 / Compare

Does the comparison support the claim?

Who and what you test limits what you can conclude. Random sampling concerns who enters the study, so it governs how far results generalize. Random assignment concerns which condition each person or task receives, so it governs whether a difference can be attributed to the change.

MethodWhat it can supportWatch for
Randomized A/B testA causal estimate for the population tested, when well designedAssignment errors, spillover between groups, too few units
HoldoutIncremental impact against a continuing baselineKeeping the holdout representative and untouched
Before / afterA first signal when randomizing isn’t practicalSeasonality, changing task difficulty, growing user experience, other changes shipped at the same time
Interviews, usability studiesWhy something works or fails: problems and mechanismsNot causal magnitude on their own

Example (hypothetical, Tastemake): Enthusiastic early adopters who opted into a new feature may rate recommendations higher than people who never chose to use it, whatever the feature itself does.

Mistake to avoid: Assuming a large sample fixes selection bias or missing outcomes. More data from a skewed group gives a more precise answer to the wrong question.

04 / Interpret

How convincing is the difference?

Read three things together: how big the change is, how uncertain that estimate is, and whether a change that size matters in practice.

TermPlain meaningIt does not mean
Effect sizeHow much the outcome changed, such as 4 percentage points or 2 minutes per taskThat the change is worth its cost
Confidence intervalA range of effects consistent with the data: sampling uncertainty under the analysis assumptionsProtection against bias, poor measurement or a skewed sample
p-value and statistical significanceHow unusual results at least this extreme would be if there were no real difference; “significant” means below a threshold chosen in advanceThe probability the hypothesis is true, or proof the effect is important or causal
PowerThe probability of detecting a specified real effect under the planned designA guarantee for effects smaller than the one specified
Minimum detectable effectThe effect the test is designed to detect at its chosen powerA universal sample size: the right size depends on variability, the effect that matters and the design

Example: A small improvement can be statistically significant with enough data, yet too small to justify the additional cost.

Mistake to avoid: Reading “not statistically significant” as “the options are equivalent.” Often the test simply could not tell. Likewise, zero observed failures does not establish zero risk: 0 failures in 30 cases is still consistent with a real failure rate as high as about 10%.

05 / AI evals

What changes for AI evaluations?

When comparing configurations (model, prompt, retrieval), run them on the same cases where appropriate and keep the pairing: compare case by case and inspect which cases improved and which regressed, not just the two totals.

Different scenarios test coverage; repeated trials of one scenario test consistency. Report unique cases separately from the number of runs. Check the grader as well: does it apply a valid rubric? Inspect where it disagrees with human judgment, especially severe false passes. Agreement with human grading and probability calibration (whether a stated 80% confidence is right about 80% of the time) are different properties. After tuning, confirm on fresh held-out cases you did not tune on.

Example: Two configurations both raise a pass rate from 80% to 84%. One fixes 4 cases and breaks none; the other fixes 12 and breaks 8, including a severe one. The totals match; the decisions should not.

Mistake to avoid: Treating many outputs from a few underlying scenarios as independent evidence about many different tasks.

06 / Decide

What decision does the evidence justify?

Check the measurement pipeline before interpreting results. Missing events, exclusions applied differently to each group, assignment counts that don’t match the planned split, repeated significance checks that stop when a result appears, and searching many metrics for one that moved can all create misleading findings.

Then connect the result to total workflow effort, useful capacity, operating cost, risk and remaining uncertainty. A reasonable next step may be a limited test, a revision, a careful expansion, or stopping.

Example: Faster drafting does not demonstrate labor savings if review takes all the saved time.

Mistake to avoid: Choosing the most favorable metric after seeing the results and presenting it as the original success criterion.

GOING DEEPER

Plans, value and the business case

A one-page plan before you test

Write these down before collecting results, so the success criterion cannot drift afterwards.

  • Decision and hypothesis: what you will do differently depending on the result.
  • Baseline: current workflow, simpler alternative or existing configuration.
  • Primary metric: numerator, denominator, eligibility, exclusions, window.
  • Guardrails: severe failures, review burden, cost, latency, accessibility.
  • Worthwhile effect: the smallest change that would justify acting, and whether the planned test can detect it.
  • Signals over time: leading signals (early use) versus lagging outcomes (resolution, retention, rework).
  • Unit and assignment: users, accounts or tasks, and how each gets its condition.
  • Other explanations: what else could produce the same movement.
Quality × value: decide what earns investment

Use separate evidence for AI task quality and real workflow value. The matrix is a decision aid, not permission to waive safety, privacy or compliance requirements.

AI qualityProduct valueLikely next decision
StrongStrongExpand carefully; monitor quality, cost and adoption by segment.
WeakStrongFix quality and constrain exposure; block launch or expansion for critical failures.
StrongWeakRevisit the job, workflow fit, discovery and adoption rather than optimizing eval scores.
WeakWeakStop or fundamentally redesign the proposed feature.
Strong quality, unproven value: A support assistant produces 90% acceptable drafts. Compare assisted and baseline cases on actual resolution, total agent effort including corrections, escalation, customer satisfaction and cost per case. If resolution is unchanged and correction time rises, the quality score has not established business value.
Non-negotiable: Strong usage never excuses critical safety failures; strong evals never prove a feature is needed.
Is it worth it? A simple AI business case

Start from what the problem costs today, not from what the model can do. Every number below is an assumption until measured: label it as one, and keep the case simple enough that someone can check it.

LineWhat to estimateWatch for
Baseline painVolume × time per case × loaded cost, plus the cost of errors and rework in today's workflow.Without a reliable baseline, the value case stays provisional. Measure it before claiming an improvement or committing to a major rollout.
ValueTime saved that becomes useful capacity, errors avoided, faster cycle time, revenue or retention effects.Time saved creates capacity, not automatic savings. Count it when it shows up as more completed work, avoided overtime or hiring, better service or less rework, and keep those gains separate from cash savings.
Run costModel and API, retrieval and infrastructure, priced per successful outcome.Retries, failures and long inputs; the cost per call understates it.
Hidden costHuman review, exception handling, evals and monitoring, upkeep as models and prompts change.Review burden often erases the headline saving.
RiskImpact × likelihood of a wrong answer or action, compliance exposure, loss of trust.One critical failure can outweigh a year of savings.
Net value / month = value − run cost − hidden costPayback = build cost ÷ net value per monthCompare with the simplest alternative: rules, automation or a process change
Make the recommendation falsifiable: state the decision (build, limited pilot, buy, or don't), the two or three assumptions it is most sensitive to, and the evidence that would change it. To find them, ask what must happen operationally for the productivity gain to become business value: faster drafts, then faster resolution, then more cases without added staff, then a smaller backlog. Each link is an assumption to test. A case that cannot be wrong cannot guide a decision.

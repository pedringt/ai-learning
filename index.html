Foundation

How LLMs behave

Build a practical mental model of tokens, next-token prediction, context, probabilities, variability, and hallucination. Understand why an LLM can produce a fluent answer without actually verifying that the answer is true.

  • Model knowledge vs. information supplied at runtime
  • Why the same prompt can produce different outputs
  • Why confidence of tone is not confidence of fact
  • How these limits shape product design and customer expectations
  • Recognize hallucination, bias, and sycophancy as distinct failure patterns
Practice checkpoint

Explain to a customer why a fluent answer can still be wrong without describing the model as “random” or “just guessing.”

Foundation

4D AI Fluency

Use Anthropic's 4D framework as a practical model for strong human-AI collaboration: decide what to delegate, describe the task well, discern the quality of the result, and use diligence around responsibility and impact.

  • Delegation: choose what work AI should and should not do
  • Description: provide context, instructions, examples, and success criteria
  • Discernment: judge quality, limits, uncertainty, and evidence
  • Diligence: protect privacy, disclose use appropriately, and retain human accountability
Practice checkpoint

Take one real work task and describe how each of the four Ds changes your approach from start to finish.

Foundation

Prompting & Delegation

Treat prompting as task design rather than clever wording. Define the job, give useful context, set constraints, specify the output, and decide how the result will be checked.

  • Task → Context → Constraints → Output → Verify
  • Break complex work into smaller steps when useful
  • Use examples when desired behavior is hard to describe
  • Iterate based on failure instead of endlessly rewriting prompts
  • Use the Description → Discernment loop: improve the instructions, inspect the output, revise based on evidence
Practice checkpoint

Take one vague request and improve the delegation, then explain which change should improve reliability and why.

Foundation

Context & Knowledge

Understand where an AI answer is actually getting its information. Separate conversation context, uploaded files, persistent project knowledge, connectors, retrieval/RAG, and tool/API results.

  • Ask what source should be authoritative
  • Know that stale or conflicting knowledge cannot be fixed by prompting
  • Understand context-window limits and what may be omitted
  • Connect source quality to output quality
  • Use Projects, uploaded knowledge, and connectors as hands-on examples of reusable context
Practice checkpoint

Given a wrong policy answer, identify at least four different information paths that could have produced it and what evidence would distinguish them.

Foundation

Claude Product Fluency

Practice choosing the Claude interface or capability that best matches the work while keeping the underlying lesson platform-neutral.

  • Projects: persistent instructions and reusable knowledge
  • Artifacts: longer or reusable outputs that benefit from an editable workspace
  • Research: multi-source investigation where evidence and synthesis matter
  • Skills: reusable instructions or repeatable ways of working
  • Connectors: bring organizational knowledge into the workflow with explicit permissions and source boundaries
  • Cowork: delegate larger multi-step work that spans files, tools, and substantial deliverables
Practice checkpoint

For three different work tasks, choose chat, Project, Artifact, Research, a connector-supported workflow, or Cowork and explain the decision.

Foundation

Model & Product Selection

Choose the simplest approach that can reliably do the job. Think in universal tradeoffs rather than memorizing product names.

  • Capability / reasoning quality
  • Latency and user experience
  • Cost at expected volume
  • Context and knowledge requirements
  • Chat vs. reusable workspace vs. research vs. delegated multi-step work
Practice checkpoint

Compare a high-volume classification task with a complex strategy synthesis task and recommend a different model/workflow profile for each.

Foundation

Output Evaluation & Validation

Learn to judge whether an answer is useful, grounded, complete, and safe—not merely well written. Separate factual claims, inference, opinion, and generated recommendations.

  • Verify important claims against authoritative sources
  • Define what “good” means before evaluating
  • Look for unsupported assumptions and missing information
  • Use human review where the cost of error is high
  • Watch for sycophancy: agreement with the user is not evidence that the conclusion is sound
Practice checkpoint

Review one output for factuality, completeness, source grounding, usefulness, risk, and whether the model is simply mirroring the user's framing.

Foundation

Responsible Use & Governance

Recognize where AI use creates privacy, security, bias, compliance, or decision-risk concerns. Know when to stop experimenting and involve the right specialist.

  • Sensitive data and permissions
  • High-impact or irreversible decisions
  • Blast radius: what can the AI actually change, delete, send, or expose if it is wrong?
  • Bias and inappropriate automation
  • Organizational policy and auditability
  • Escalation to Security, Legal, Engineering, or domain experts
  • Human accountability even when AI contributes heavily to the work
Practice checkpoint

For a proposed HR or financial workflow, separate what AI may assist with from what requires policy, specialist review, or human ownership.

Foundation

AI Diligence & Disclosure

Practice documenting how AI contributed to a deliverable, which sources were authoritative, what was independently checked, what limitations remain, and who owns the final judgment.

  • What AI did
  • What data or sources it used
  • What a human verified or changed
  • What uncertainty remains
  • Who is accountable for the final output or decision
Practice checkpoint

Write a short diligence statement for one AI-assisted analysis or portfolio artifact without overstating either AI or human contribution.

Foundation

Behavior-Based Fluency

Track what you can reliably do, not just what you have read. Fluency should show up as repeatable judgment in unfamiliar situations.

  • Notice when missing context is likely to matter
  • Choose an authoritative source instead of asking the model to guess
  • Revise the workflow when the same failure repeats
  • Know when to verify, escalate, or stop
  • Explain why an output should or should not be trusted
Practice checkpoint

For each major lesson, record one observable behavior that would prove you can apply it in a new scenario.

Context Drift & Stale Decisions

I’ve already seen a long-running Cowork project drift outside the useful context: it started repeating older decisions and moving in a direction that no longer matched the current plan.

  • Recognize when the working context is no longer trustworthy
  • Re-anchor the task to current decisions instead of letting stale assumptions compound
  • Review outputs during execution instead of only at the end
  • Treat context management as an operating concern, not just a model limitation

Blast Radius & Consequential Actions

I’ve also had to think about permissions when Cowork could take consequential actions on my computer, including deleting files. That makes “what can the agent actually change?” a practical question, not an abstract safety concept.

  • Pause before destructive or hard-to-reverse actions
  • Separate drafting/recommendation from execution when the risk is higher
  • Think about recovery if the action is wrong

Technical fluency

Model vs. AI Application

The model is only one layer. A production AI experience may also include system instructions, retrieved context, deterministic business logic, tools, permissions, routing, UI, and human review.

  • Do not blame “the model” before identifying the failed layer
  • Trace where information and decisions entered the workflow
Practice checkpoint

Draw a simple system around one AI feature and label which behavior comes from the model versus application logic.

Technical fluency

Retrieval & Grounding

Understand retrieval/RAG as one important way systems ground answers in current, private, or domain-specific knowledge—not as the whole architecture.

  • Retrieval quality vs. generation quality
  • Freshness, metadata, chunking, filters, conflicts
  • Correct context retrieved but ignored
  • Know when a simpler connector or direct context path is enough
Practice checkpoint

Diagnose three failures: wrong document retrieved, correct document retrieved but answer wrong, and authoritative knowledge itself stale.

Technical fluency

Agentic Architecture

Distinguish a single model call, a fixed multi-step workflow, a tool-using agent, and coordinated agent systems. Understand why more autonomy also creates more failure modes.

  • When does this actually need an agent?
  • What should remain deterministic?
  • Where should humans approve or intervene?
  • What is the blast radius if the agent is wrong?
Practice checkpoint

Take one use case and argue for the least-agentic architecture that can still meet the business requirement.

Technical fluency

Tool Use

Tools let the AI retrieve live data or take actions in external systems. Understand the full loop: model decision → tool request → external system → result → next model step.

  • Wrong tool or wrong parameters
  • Permission and authentication failures
  • Timeouts, partial failures, ambiguous success
  • Higher guardrails for irreversible actions
  • Minimum permissions and recovery paths for consequential actions
Practice checkpoint

Trace what should happen when a refund tool times out after an uncertain execution state and explain why blind retry may be unsafe.

Technical fluency

MCP Concepts

Learn MCP as a standardized way for AI systems to discover and use external tools and data sources. Focus on where it sits in the architecture and how failures surface—not how to build a server.

  • Client, server, tool/data source relationship
  • Permissions and trust boundaries
  • How MCP fits into troubleshooting
Practice checkpoint

Explain MCP to a nontechnical customer in one minute, then to an engineer in one minute, emphasizing different details.

Technical fluency

Context Management

Production systems have to decide what information belongs in the model's limited working context and what can be summarized, retrieved later, or omitted.

  • Long conversations and state
  • Passing context between workflow steps
  • Summarization loss and stale context
  • Cost/latency tradeoffs of large context
  • Recognize context drift from real delegated-work experience
Practice checkpoint

Design what context should survive a long customer conversation and what should be retrieved fresh rather than carried forever.

Technical fluency

Structured Outputs

Use free-form text when humans are the consumer; use structured fields when software must reliably consume the result.

  • Classification fields and schemas
  • JSON / structured records
  • Validation before downstream action
Practice checkpoint

Convert one prose AI result into a schema that another system could safely validate before acting on it.

Technical fluency

Reliability Patterns

Production AI should not rely on “hopefully the model behaves.” Learn the controls wrapped around probabilistic behavior.

  • Validators, retries, and fallbacks
  • Risk- or evidence-based routing thresholds
  • Do not treat a model's self-reported confidence as a calibrated probability
  • Source/provenance checks
  • Human-in-the-loop approval
  • Regression testing after changes
Practice checkpoint

Wrap a risky model decision in at least three reliability controls and explain which failure each control addresses.

Technical fluency

Traces & Observability

Use traces to reconstruct what actually happened across model calls, retrieval, tools, routing, and handoffs instead of diagnosing from the final answer alone.

  • Input → context → model → tool → output
  • Latency and error signals
  • Root-cause evidence for Engineering
Practice checkpoint

Read a simulated trace and produce a concise engineering escalation: symptom, likely layer, evidence, business impact, and reproduction case.

From “we want AI” to measurable customer value

The version of AI Customer Success I’m preparing for sits between customer goals, product capability, technical reality, and measurable outcomes. It is more than teaching features: the work is helping customers choose the right use case, get it into a real workflow, build trust and adoption, troubleshoot what breaks, and show whether it is actually creating value.

01 · Discover

Discovery & Use-Case Qualification

Start with the problem, not the AI feature the customer thinks they need. If someone asks for “an agent,” I still want to understand whether the real issue is slow work, inconsistent decisions, poor knowledge access, manual data entry, or something else.

  • Problem: What is painful today?
  • Outcome: What measurable change would matter?
  • Workflow: Who owns the process and what happens before/after?
  • Risk: What is the cost of a wrong answer or action?
  • Knowledge: What data is required, where is it, and who owns it?
  • Human role: Assist, recommend, automate, or require approval?
  • Economics: Volume, latency, quality, and cost constraints?
Practice output

Run a 30-minute discovery simulation and produce a one-page use-case brief with problem, stakeholders, workflow, data, risks, success metrics, and recommendation.

02 · Evaluate

Define “Good” Before Launch

I want to define what “good” looks like before rollout instead of waiting for users to tell us something feels off. Vague goals like “helpful” or “accurate” need to become behavior we can test consistently.

  • Core cases, edge cases, ambiguous inputs, tool failures, safety cases
  • Expected behavior specific enough two reviewers would agree
  • Pass/fail thresholds and severity
  • Correct source/tool use—not only a plausible final answer
  • Regression checks when prompts, models, knowledge, or tools change
Practice output

Build a compact eval plan with test categories, pass criteria, severity, expected source/tool behavior, and launch-blocking thresholds.

02 · Troubleshoot

Find Which Layer Failed

A bad AI answer does not automatically mean the model is the problem. I want to figure out which part of the system actually failed before jumping to a fix.

  • Input quality or ambiguity
  • Retrieval/context failure
  • Instructions/model behavior
  • Tool/API failure
  • Business logic or routing
  • Permissions or identity
  • Human handoff / UI integration
Practice output

Write an escalation with customer symptom, business impact, reproduction steps, trace evidence, likely failure layer, and the specific question for Engineering.

03 · Enable

Trust, Training & Adoption

Even a technically good workflow can fail if people do not trust it, understand it, or see how it fits into their real work. Good enablement has to cover both what the AI can do and where its limits are.

  • What AI is for—and what it is not for
  • How users verify, correct, and escalate
  • Safe-use guidance for risky categories
  • Workflow-specific training rather than generic prompt tips
  • Adoption, friction, and confidence feedback after rollout
Practice output

Create a launch kit: training agenda, quick-start guide, safe-use guide, and a short confidence/friction survey.

03 · Change

Change Management & Resistance

If adoption is low, I do not want to assume people just need more training. The problem could be trust, extra review work, weak workflow fit, lack of manager reinforcement, or real product friction.

  • Identify champions, skeptics, and workflow owners
  • Separate trust problems from usability and value problems
  • Use pilot feedback to change the workflow—not only the training
  • Set explicit norms for when to rely, verify, correct, or escalate
  • Watch for shadow workflows that bypass the intended process
Practice output

Create a resistance diagnosis for a pilot with low adoption: evidence to collect, likely root causes, intervention by segment, and the signal that would show the intervention worked.

04 · Operate

Run the Customer Relationship

Once a workflow is live, I want to look at the whole picture: system signals, human feedback, and business context. Healthy usage should mean the workflow is actually helping, not just that people logged in.

  • Usage and adoption patterns
  • Quality and human-intervention trends
  • Incidents and recurring failure themes
  • Knowledge freshness and configuration drift
  • Stakeholder confidence and workflow friction
Practice output

Review a simulated 90-day account dataset and produce health, risks, priorities, stakeholder actions, and an operating cadence.

05 · Measure

Prove Business Value

For me, usage alone is not enough. I want to know whether the AI is working well, how much human correction it still needs, and whether that ultimately changes a business outcome.

  • Adoption: who is using it and where?
  • Quality: does it behave correctly?
  • Human intervention: how much correction or escalation is required?
  • Outcome: did speed, quality, revenue, cost, or experience improve?
  • Economics: is the value worth the model/tool/operating cost?
Practice output

Build a success review that distinguishes leading indicators from business outcomes and makes a clear scale, change, or stop recommendation.

05 · Economics

Cost & ROI Modeling

I want to be able to connect technical choices to costs a customer actually cares about, including the human work that remains around the AI—not just the model bill.

  • Baseline labor cost and current throughput
  • Model, tool, retrieval, and platform cost at expected volume
  • Human review, correction, and exception-handling cost
  • Time/capacity benefit and avoided cost
  • Break-even volume and sensitivity to quality or review rate
  • ROI = value created or cost avoided relative to total operating cost
Practice output

Model a simple pilot with baseline labor cost, AI cost per transaction, review time, adoption, and error/rework. Show the break-even point and which assumption matters most.

Decision support

Competitive & Product Fit Conversations

I want to be able to help customers compare AI products without turning into a brand cheerleader. The recommendation should come back to their use case, constraints, evidence, and operating environment.

  • Capability and task fit
  • Reliability, controllability, and evaluation evidence
  • Security, governance, privacy, and deployment requirements
  • Integrations, ecosystem, workflow fit, and switching burden
  • Latency, usability, support model, and total cost
  • Run a proof against customer-defined success criteria when claims are close
Practice output

Respond to “Why should we use Claude instead of another AI product?” with a discovery-led comparison that avoids unsupported competitor claims and recommends a fair evaluation process.

Questions that changed the project

The final artifacts matter, but so does the reasoning that changed them. These are the kinds of questions that forced me to revise assumptions instead of simply polishing the original plan.

Question 01Are reps actually working this way often enough for this metric to mean anything?

The answer weakened my original quick-first-touch assumption and changed the measurement model.

Question 02Are we asking users to create data manually that the system already captures more reliably?

This pushed me away from fragile manual workflow tagging and toward existing timestamp evidence.

Question 03Is this bad output a model problem, or is the source knowledge itself wrong?

Stale and conflicting knowledge turned source ownership and freshness into prerequisites, not prompt-tuning details.

Question 04Does this workflow need AI at all, or is there a simpler product or configuration fix?

A copy/paste pain point became evidence for choosing the simplest reliable fix rather than forcing another AI integration.

Question 05What happens if the AI is wrong here—what can it actually change, expose, or cost?

That risk lens helped define hard boundaries around money, security, evidence, and mandatory human review.

Question 06Which parts of this process should remain deterministic or human-owned?

The answer shaped the architecture: deterministic risk routing, retrieval where knowledge is needed, AI-assisted drafting, and human decision authority.

Question 07What evidence would make me change this design?

The working tracker keeps assumptions, simulated stakeholder answers, rejected ideas, and decision changes visible so the project does not look falsely linear.

AI Workbench · Breadth

Small problems. Transferable judgment.

Meridian proves depth. The Workbench proves you can walk into a different department, discover the real problem, design the right AI role, identify risk, and define success.

›Marketing

Launch Content

Turn source material into campaign copy without inventing product claims.

Customer need:

Reduce launch-content turnaround without creating factual or brand risk.

Discovery:

Which sources are authoritative? Which claims require Product/Legal approval? Which channels and audiences matter?

AI role:

Synthesize approved source material, draft channel-specific copy, and flag claims that need verification.

Measure:

Draft cycle time, reviewer rework, unsupported-claim rate, and adoption by the marketing team.

›Project / Product

Meeting → Plan

Turn messy transcripts into decisions, actions, owners, dependencies, and risks.

Customer need:

Stop losing decisions and follow-ups across recurring project meetings.

Discovery:

What counts as a committed action? Can the AI assign an owner if none was explicitly stated? Where should actions be stored?

AI role:

Extract explicit decisions/actions, separate uncertain inferences, and produce a structured review queue.

Measure:

Admin time saved, correction rate, missed commitments, and owner acceptance.

›HR

Policy Knowledge

Answer employee questions using authoritative internal policy sources.

Customer need:

Give employees faster access to policy information without turning AI into an HR decision-maker.

Discovery:

Which policies are authoritative? How often do they change? What data is sensitive? Which questions must go to HR?

AI role:

Retrieve current policy, answer with citations, and escalate ambiguous, personal, or high-impact cases.

Measure:

Grounded-answer rate, stale-source incidents, HR deflection, and employee confidence.

›Sales

Account Intelligence

Summarize calls and identify themes without fabricating customer intent.

Customer need:

Reduce post-call admin and improve account visibility across the revenue team.

Discovery:

Which CRM fields matter? What may be inferred versus directly stated? Which commercial judgments remain human-owned?

AI role:

Summarize evidence, extract explicit commitments, propose structured CRM updates, and label hypotheses clearly.

Measure:

Follow-up time, CRM completeness, correction rate, and unsupported-inference rate.

›Operations

Workflow Redesign

Decide what AI should assist, automate, leave deterministic, or leave human-owned.

Customer need:

Improve a high-volume manual process without adding unnecessary agent complexity.

Discovery:

Where is the actual bottleneck? Which decisions are rules-based? Which actions are reversible? What is the error cost?

AI role:

Use AI only for ambiguous language/reasoning steps; keep deterministic checks and sensitive approvals outside the model.

Measure:

Cycle time, error rate, exception volume, automation rate, operating cost, and human intervention.

›Customer Success

Health & Risk

Turn account signals into evidence-backed risk hypotheses and next steps.

Customer need:

Help CSMs identify meaningful risk earlier without creating opaque automated health judgments.

Discovery:

Which signals are reliable? What does “at risk” mean? Which data is missing? Who owns commercial decisions?

AI role:

Synthesize usage, support, feedback, and account context; distinguish evidence from hypotheses; recommend questions and next steps.

Measure:

Prep time, useful-risk detection, false alarms, CSM adoption, and whether interventions improve outcomes.

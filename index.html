01AI Systems & FoundationsUnderstand how modern AI systems behave, what sits around the model, and where failures come from.LLM behavior · context engineering · grounding/RAG · tools · agents · MCP · structured outputs · reliability

Core

How LLMs behave

Probabilistic generation, context dependence, hallucination, instruction following, uncertainty, and practical model limitations.

Model vs. AI application

Separate model behavior from retrieval, instructions, tools, orchestration, data, permissions, and product logic.

Context engineering

Decide what instructions, retrieved evidence, tools, state, prior results, and other context the model receives at each step—and keep that context relevant, trustworthy, and proportionate to the task.

Retrieval & grounding

RAG, source selection, freshness, metadata, conflicting sources, citations, and when simpler context injection is enough. Retrieval should be tested against the real query set; the right design may combine semantic and keyword search, metadata filters, reranking, or safe fallback rather than assuming one search method is universally best. Applied in Meridian →

Tool use & structured outputs

Actions, schemas, validation, permissions, failures, recovery, and reliable handoffs between model output and software.

Agentic systems & harnesses

Autonomy levels, task decomposition, state/memory, stopping conditions, verification, delegation, error recovery, and when an agent is the wrong architecture. Treat the harness—context management, tools, permissions, orchestration, and recovery—as part of the system being designed and evaluated.

Reference / deeper dive

MCP & tool protocols

How models connect to tools and context through standardized interfaces; understand the architecture without treating one protocol as permanent.

Orchestration & production tradeoffs

Routing, fallbacks, caching, latency, reliability, model choice, cost/performance, and state across longer workflows.

Practice

Explain an AI application as a system rather than “the model”; diagnose whether a failure came from instructions, retrieval, tools, permissions, context, or generation; decide whether the best response is AI, a hybrid design, conventional software, process/data repair, or no build.

Applied AI delivery

From “we want AI” to measurable business value

Applied AI work sits between business goals, user workflows, product capability, technical reality, and measurable outcomes. It is not just using a model or teaching features. The work includes choosing the right use case, fitting AI into a real workflow, supporting appropriate trust and adoption, diagnosing failures, and showing whether the system is actually creating value.

01 · Discover

Discovery & Use-Case Qualification

Start with the problem, not the AI feature the customer thinks they need. If someone asks for “an agent,” I still want to understand whether the real issue is slow work, inconsistent decisions, poor knowledge access, manual data entry, or something else.

  • Problem: What is painful today?
  • Outcome: What measurable change would matter?
  • Workflow: Who owns the process and what happens before/after?
  • Risk: What is the cost of a wrong answer or action?
  • Knowledge: What data is required, where is it, and who owns it?
  • Human role: Assist, recommend, automate, or require approval?
  • Economics: Volume, latency, quality, and cost constraints?
Practice output

Run a 30-minute discovery simulation and produce a one-page use-case brief with problem, stakeholders, workflow, data, risks, success metrics, and recommendation.

02 · Evaluate

Define “Good” Before Launch

I want to define what “good” looks like before rollout instead of waiting for users to tell us something feels off. Vague goals like “helpful” or “accurate” need to become behavior we can test consistently.

  • Core cases, edge cases, ambiguous inputs, tool failures, safety cases
  • Expected behavior specific enough two reviewers would agree
  • Pass/fail thresholds and severity
  • Correct source/tool use—not only a plausible final answer
  • Regression checks when prompts, models, knowledge, or tools change
Practice output

Build a compact eval plan with test categories, pass criteria, severity, expected source/tool behavior, and launch-blocking thresholds.

02 · Troubleshoot

Find Which Layer Failed

A bad AI answer does not automatically mean the model is the problem. I want to figure out which part of the system actually failed before jumping to a fix.

  • Input quality or ambiguity
  • Retrieval/context failure
  • Instructions/model behavior
  • Tool/API failure
  • Business logic or routing
  • Permissions or identity
  • Human handoff / UI integration
Practice output

Write an escalation with customer symptom, business impact, reproduction steps, trace evidence, likely failure layer, and the specific question for Engineering.

03 · Enable

Trust, Training & Adoption

Even a technically good workflow can fail if people do not trust it, understand it, or see how it fits into their real work. Good enablement has to cover both what the AI can do and where its limits are.

  • What AI is for—and what it is not for
  • How users verify, correct, and escalate
  • Safe-use guidance for risky categories
  • Workflow-specific training rather than generic prompt tips
  • Adoption, friction, and confidence feedback after rollout
Practice output

Create a launch kit: training agenda, quick-start guide, safe-use guide, and a short confidence/friction survey.

03 · Change

Change Management & Resistance

Low adoption does not automatically mean people need more training. The cause could be low trust, added review work, poor workflow fit, weak manager reinforcement, or real product friction.

  • Identify champions, skeptics, and workflow owners
  • Separate trust problems from usability and value problems
  • Use pilot feedback to change the workflow—not only the training
  • Set explicit norms for when to rely, verify, correct, or escalate
  • Watch for shadow workflows that bypass the intended process
Practice output

Create a resistance diagnosis for a pilot with low adoption: evidence to collect, likely root causes, intervention by segment, and the signal that would show the intervention worked.

04 · Operate

Run the Customer Relationship

Once a workflow is live, evaluate the whole picture: system behavior, human feedback, and business context. Healthy usage is not enough if the workflow is not actually helping.

  • Usage and adoption patterns
  • Quality and human-intervention trends
  • Incidents and recurring failure themes
  • Knowledge freshness and configuration drift
  • Stakeholder confidence and workflow friction
Practice output

Review a simulated 90-day account dataset and produce health, risks, priorities, stakeholder actions, and an operating cadence.

05 · Measure

Prove Business Value

For me, usage alone is not enough. I want to know whether the AI is working well, how much human correction it still needs, and whether that ultimately changes a business outcome.

  • Adoption: who is using it and where?
  • Quality: does it behave correctly?
  • Human intervention: how much correction or escalation is required?
  • Outcome: did speed, quality, revenue, cost, or experience improve?
  • Economics: is the value worth the model/tool/operating cost?
Practice output

Build a success review that distinguishes leading indicators from business outcomes and makes a clear scale, change, or stop recommendation.

05 · Economics

Cost & ROI Modeling

I want to be able to connect technical choices to costs a customer actually cares about, including the human work that remains around the AI—not just the model bill.

  • Baseline labor cost and current throughput
  • Model, tool, retrieval, and platform cost at expected volume
  • Human review, correction, and exception-handling cost
  • Time/capacity benefit and avoided cost
  • Break-even volume and sensitivity to quality or review rate
  • ROI = value created or cost avoided relative to total operating cost
Practice output

Model a simple pilot with baseline labor cost, AI cost per transaction, review time, adoption, and error/rework. Show the break-even point and which assumption matters most.

Decision support

Competitive & Product Fit Conversations

Compare AI products without becoming a brand cheerleader. Recommendations should come back to the customer’s actual use case, constraints, available evidence, and operating environment.

  • Capability and task fit
  • Reliability, controllability, and evaluation evidence
  • Security, governance, privacy, and deployment requirements
  • Integrations, ecosystem, workflow fit, and switching burden
  • Latency, usability, support model, and total cost
  • Run a proof against customer-defined success criteria when claims are close
Practice output

Respond to “Why should we use Claude instead of another AI product?” with a discovery-led comparison that avoids unsupported competitor claims and recommends a fair evaluation process.

Communication practice

Explaining the pilot without overselling it

This mock stakeholder brief translates the operating design into a clear promise, explicit boundaries, and an evidence-based expansion gate.

Customer update · practice artifact
01

The pilot promise

What we are testing: an eight-week AI-assisted Tier 1 pilot intended to improve first-response time without weakening response quality or customer trust.

What changes: eligible low-risk tickets can be classified, grounded in approved knowledge, and drafted with AI. A rep reviews every response.

02

The boundaries

Money, security, and other higher-risk requests continue directly to a human. The pilot is not testing autonomous support.

I would not claim
  • That results have already improved.
  • That AI will solve the backlog.
  • That human review is temporary.
03

The expansion gate

How we judge it: response time, CSAT, grounding, escalation behavior, rep adoption, and failure patterns must be reviewed together.

Expansion would require faster eligible-ticket handling without degraded customer experience, unacceptable risk, or unmanageable review burden.

Evidence boundary

Meridian is fictional and this brief is practice material. AI helped me iterate on the wording; the portfolio evidence is the judgment behind what I emphasize, what I refuse to promise, and what proof I require before expansion.

›HR

Policy Knowledge

Answer employee questions using authoritative internal policy sources.

Customer need:

Give employees faster access to policy information without turning AI into an HR decision-maker.

Discovery:

Which policies are authoritative? How often do they change? What data is sensitive? Which questions must go to HR?

AI role:

Retrieve current policy, answer with citations, and escalate ambiguous, personal, or high-impact cases.

Grounded-answer rate, stale-source incidents, HR deflection, and employee confidence.

Reduce post-call admin and improve account visibility across the revenue team.

Discovery:

Which CRM fields matter? What may be inferred versus directly stated? Which commercial judgments remain human-owned?

AI role:

Summarize evidence, extract explicit commitments, propose structured CRM updates, and label hypotheses clearly.

Measure:

Follow-up time, CRM completeness, correction rate, and unsupported-inference rate.

AI role:

Synthesize usage, support, feedback, and account context; distinguish evidence from hypotheses; recommend questions and next steps.

Measure:

Prep time, useful-risk detection, false alarms, CSM adoption, and whether interventions improve outcomes.

The site itself is the portfolio. Applied Work lets you browse by case; Capabilities lets you browse the same work by capability.

Depth · primary case study

Meridian AI Support Pilot

One simulated B2B SaaS support implementation followed deeply through discovery, measurement, workflow design, guardrails, evaluation, adoption, operations, and value realization.

Use Meridian when you want to see:

How the pieces of an AI implementation connect over time, including the decisions and tradeoffs behind the polished artifacts.

Capabilities demonstrated: Discovery & Workflow · Measurement & Value · Systems & Guardrails · Evals & Quality

Behind the work: the Working Tracker is Meridian’s process record—questions, assumptions, reasoning, revisions, and changes of mind. It is not a separate project.

Breadth · transfer practice

Workbench · Smaller Cases

The Workbench is the collection of smaller Applied Work cases. These exercises test whether the same judgment transfers beyond Meridian into different workflows, business functions, industries, and levels of AI autonomy.

Use the Workbench when you want to see:

How I approach a new problem without assuming the answer is “use more AI.” Harborstone invoice operations is the first completed case.

Capabilities demonstrated in Harborstone: Discovery & Workflow · Measurement & Value · Systems & Guardrails · Evals & Quality

Future domains: business/operations exercises, agentic work, and a legal-AI adoption scenario that can combine general AI implementation skills with domain knowledge.

01AI Systems & Product FoundationsUnderstand how modern AI systems behave, what sits around the model, and where failures come from.LLM behavior · context engineering · grounding/RAG · tools · agents · MCP · structured outputs · reliability

Core

How LLMs behave

Probabilistic generation, context dependence, hallucination, instruction following, uncertainty, and practical model limitations.

Model vs. AI application

Separate model behavior from retrieval, instructions, tools, orchestration, data, permissions, and product logic.

Context engineering

Decide what instructions, retrieved evidence, tools, state, prior results, and other context the model receives at each step—and keep that context relevant, trustworthy, and proportionate to the task.

Retrieval & grounding

RAG, source selection, freshness, metadata, conflicting sources, citations, and when simpler context injection is enough. Retrieval should be tested against the real query set; the right design may combine semantic and keyword search, metadata filters, reranking, or safe fallback rather than assuming one search method is universally best. Applied in Meridian →

How retrieval works
Question→Embed & search→Relevant sources→Add to context→LLM→Answer

The question and stored documents both get converted into numerical vectors; retrieval finds the closest matches by similarity and hands them to the model as context — instead of relying only on what the model learned during training.

Tool use & structured outputs

Actions, schemas, validation, permissions, failures, recovery, and reliable handoffs between model output and software.

Agentic systems & harnesses

Autonomy levels, task decomposition, state/memory, stopping conditions, verification, delegation, error recovery, and when an agent is the wrong architecture. Treat the harness—context management, tools, permissions, orchestration, and recovery—as part of the system being designed and evaluated.

Reference / deeper dive

MCP & tool protocols

How models connect to tools and context through standardized interfaces; understand the architecture without treating one protocol as permanent.

Orchestration & production tradeoffs

Routing, fallbacks, caching, latency, reliability, model choice, cost/performance, and state across longer workflows.

Quick-reference mental models

4D AI Fluency: Delegate → Describe → Discern → Diligence. Architecture ladder: rule → model call → retrieval → workflow → tool-using agent → multi-agent only when justified — complexity must earn its way in. Output choice: inline answer → reusable artifact → structured data → source + approval for high risk.

Practice

Explain an AI application as a system rather than “the model”; diagnose whether a failure came from instructions, retrieval, tools, permissions, context, or generation; decide whether the best response is AI, a hybrid design, conventional software, process/data repair, or no build.

02Discovery, Use Cases & WorkflowsDiagnose the real problem before choosing the technology — and qualify whether it's actually worth solving.discovery · use-case qualification · workflow mapping · bottlenecks · knowledge lifecycle · AI vs rules vs humans

Core

Problem & user discovery

Stakeholder interviews, observation, assumptions, jobs/needs, constraints, current-state evidence, and separating requested solutions from underlying problems.

Discovery & use-case qualification

Start with the problem, not the AI feature someone thinks they need. If someone asks for "an agent," the real issue might be slow work, inconsistent decisions, poor knowledge access, or manual data entry. Work through problem, outcome, workflow, risk, knowledge, human role, and economics before proposing a solution.

Workflow decomposition

Map actors, systems, handoffs, bottlenecks, exceptions, failure recovery, frontstage/backstage work, and operational consequences. Applied in Harborstone →

Intervention choice

Compare AI, deterministic rules, conventional automation, process change, better information, and human judgment instead of defaulting to “use more AI.”

Knowledge systems

Ownership, lifecycle, taxonomy, freshness, source authority, conflicts, permissions, feedback signals, and maintaining trustworthy organizational knowledge.

NotebookLM / grounded document work

Analyzing a defined source set, preparing briefings, comparing requirements, and building FAQs or training material from real documents rather than open-ended prompting.

Reference / deeper dive

AI readiness & value assessment

Map the current workflow and baseline, define the value hypothesis, then score problem clarity, process stability, knowledge/data readiness, risk, human ownership, technical integration, measurement, and organizational readiness. Finish with Not Ready, Pilot Candidate, or Strong Candidate plus the blockers and evidence needed before scaling. Also applies during rollout planning — Stage 04 →

Discovery question bank

Problem, outcome, workflow, risk, knowledge/data, human role, stakeholders, scale, economics, and governance — use before solution design.

Practice

Turn an ambiguous request into discovery questions; map the workflow; identify the actual constraint; recommend where AI should and should not participate; make a recommendation with roughly 60% of the data you'd actually want, then document what you assumed to fill the gap and what specific evidence would change your mind. Run a 30-minute discovery simulation and produce a one-page use-case brief with problem, stakeholders, workflow, data, risks, success metrics, and recommendation.

03Technical Fluency & TroubleshootingRefresh the durable software concepts AI depends on, and know how to find which layer actually failed.architecture · APIs · SQL/data · version control · troubleshooting · escalation · observability · security

Core

Software architecture mental models

Frontend/backend, services, databases, queues/jobs, environments, configuration, integrations, failure boundaries, and how requests move through a system. The API asks, the backend works, the database remembers.

APIs & integrations

REST concepts, JSON, auth, headers/status codes, pagination, rate limits, webhooks, API documentation, and practical inspection with tools such as Postman.

Data & SQL refresh

Tables, keys, joins, schemas, validation, event data, analytics instrumentation, lineage, structured/unstructured data, and data-quality failure modes. Scoped to reading data, not writing production code — SELECT/JOIN queries and lineage reading, pulling validation samples, and reading an ER diagram, not DBA or ETL-writing work.

Version control & safe change

Commits, diffs, branches, and making a small change that's easy to reverse. For AI applications, treat prompts, models, retrieval settings, knowledge versions, tools, rules, and eval sets as one changeable system so a result can be reproduced and safely rolled back.

Modern software quality / QE

Risk-based testing, unit/integration/end-to-end strategy, regression automation, exploratory testing, acceptance criteria, testability, CI concepts, and the bridge from QA thinking to AI evals. Applied in Meridian →

n8n / visual workflow automation

Trigger → pass data → call a model → structure output → add human review → handle errors → send results onward. Enough hands-on use to configure a small proof of concept, not to own production automation infrastructure.

Find which layer failed

A bad AI answer doesn't automatically mean the model is the problem. Work through input quality/ambiguity, retrieval/context failure, instructions/model behavior, tool/API failure, business logic or routing, permissions or identity, and human handoff/UI integration before proposing a fix.

Evidence-rich technical escalation

Symptom → impact → reproduction steps → trace evidence → likely failure layer → owner → the exact decision or question needed from Engineering. Knowing when a problem needs to be handed off rather than solved solo.

AI and the changing software-delivery model

AI can make the coding part much faster without making discovery, integration, validation, rollout, or production risk disappear. I’m learning to estimate the whole job—not just the coding—by looking at uncertainty, integration work, consequences if something goes wrong, and how much verification is needed. A fast prototype is still very different from production-ready software. See the delivery judgment practiced in Meridian →

Reference / deeper dive

Release discipline & observability

Separate development, staging, and production; use automated checks and eval gates, feature flags or gradual rollout where useful, and retain rollback. Logs, traces, component versions, monitoring, and lineage should make it possible to reconstruct what the system actually did.

Security, privacy & data lifecycle

Authentication vs. authorization, RBAC/least privilege, secrets, PII and data classification, minimization, retention/deletion, residency, auditability, SSO/tenancy, vendor training use, and what information must never enter the system.

Reliability & graceful degradation

Design for timeouts, retries, rate limits, latency, unavailable tools, stale sources, partial failures, and cost limits. Define the fallback route and what the user sees when the AI path is unavailable.

Failure layers, quick reference

Input → retrieval/context → instructions/model → tool/API → business logic → permissions → handoff/UI. Find the layer before the fix.

Escalation template, quick reference

Symptom → impact → reproduce → evidence → likely layer → owner → decision needed. Triage, then hand off well. Also supports implementation and adoption work — Stage 04 →

Deep CI/test-automation infrastructure

Owning a CI pipeline, test-automation framework design, or release-gate infrastructure — awareness level, not a target skill right now.

Practice

Inspect an API response; write a small SQL query; diagnose a fictional production failure from logs/traces; use Git to make and safely reverse a small change; write an escalation with customer symptom, business impact, reproduction steps, trace evidence, likely failure layer, and the specific question for Engineering.

04Implementation, Onboarding & AdoptionMake a technically viable system useful, trustworthy, and sustainable for the people doing the work.rollout · onboarding · trust · review UX · training · change management · appropriate non-use

Core

Implementation planning

Pilots, rollout sequencing, dependencies, readiness, enablement, support, ownership, stakeholder alignment, and operational handoffs.

Adoption & change

Workflow fit, incentives, champions, resistance, training, appropriate non-use, repeat use, qualitative friction, and distinguishing usage from realized value.

Trust, training & adoption

Even a technically good workflow can fail if people don't trust it, understand it, or see how it fits their real work. Cover what the AI is for — and isn't — how users verify/correct/escalate, safe-use guidance for risky categories, workflow-specific training rather than generic prompt tips, and adoption/friction/confidence feedback after rollout.

Change management & resistance

Low adoption doesn't automatically mean people need more training — the cause could be low trust, added review work, poor workflow fit, weak manager reinforcement, or real product friction. Identify champions, skeptics, and workflow owners; separate trust problems from usability/value problems; watch for shadow workflows that bypass the intended process.

Trust & human factors

Automation bias, anchoring, confirmation bias, cognitive load, alert fatigue, user mental models, deskilling, accountability, and calibrated trust.

Review, control & recourse

Set appropriate expectations before use; show uncertainty, sources, or explanations when they help verification; make correction, override, escalation, undo, and recovery easy. Consequential AI-assisted decisions need a clear path for human reconsideration.

UI/UX & AI interaction design

Information architecture, user flows, usability, cognitive load, error/recovery states, progressive disclosure, onboarding, review interfaces, uncertainty, editable output, confirmations, undo, and calibrated trust.

Reference / deeper dive

Accessibility & inclusive AI use

WCAG/HCI fundamentals, different levels of AI familiarity, understandable disclosures and controls, plus journeys, service blueprints, handoffs, failure points, and recovery.

Change diagnosis, quick reference

Low usage → inspect trust, workflow fit, review burden, training, manager reinforcement, and product friction before prescribing more enablement. Adoption is a system signal, not just a training problem.

Practice

Design a pilot and adoption plan; critique a review workflow; identify likely resistance/incentives; define meaningful adoption signals; redesign an interface that encourages automation bias. Create a launch kit: training agenda, quick-start guide, safe-use guide, and a short confidence/friction survey. Diagnose a pilot with low adoption: evidence to collect, likely root causes, intervention by segment, and the signal that would show it worked.

05Customer Operations & Relationship ManagementOnce a workflow is live, evaluate the whole picture — system behavior, human feedback, and business context together.account health · operating cadence · escalation · stakeholder confidence · risk signals

Core

Run the customer relationship

Healthy usage alone isn't enough if the workflow isn't actually helping. Track usage and adoption patterns, quality and human-intervention trends, incidents and recurring failure themes, knowledge freshness and configuration drift, and stakeholder confidence and workflow friction — together, not in isolation.

Health & risk signals

Turn account signals into evidence-backed risk hypotheses and next steps. Discovery: which signals are reliable, what "at risk" actually means, what data is missing, who owns commercial decisions. AI's role: synthesize usage, support, feedback, and account context; distinguish evidence from hypotheses; recommend questions and next steps — without creating an opaque automated health score.

Success hierarchy

Adoption → quality → intervention → business outcome → economics. Never let one healthy layer hide a failing one underneath it. Also anchors measurement design — Stage 06 →

Reference / deeper dive

Escalation, applied to accounts

Same symptom → impact → reproduction → evidence → likely layer → owner shape as technical escalation, applied to account-health conversations rather than bug reports. Full template — Stage 03 →

Practice

Review a simulated 90-day account dataset and produce health, risks, priorities, stakeholder actions, and an operating cadence. Prep time, useful-risk detection, false alarms, and whether interventions actually improve outcomes are the measures that matter — not how sophisticated the health score looks.

06Measurement & Value RealizationConnect system performance to operational outcomes and determine whether the intervention is worth it.baselines · instrumentation · experiments · metrics · ROI · capacity · cost

Core

Measurement design

Baselines, metric definitions, leading/lagging indicators, instrumentation, segmentation, funnels/cohorts where useful, and avoiding vanity metrics.

Experimentation & causal thinking

Controls, confounders, selection bias, correlation vs. causation, practical vs. statistical significance, and why post-launch improvement does not prove causation.

Prove business value

Usage alone isn't enough. Track adoption (who's using it and where), quality (does it behave correctly), human intervention (how much correction/escalation is required), outcome (did speed, quality, revenue, cost, or experience improve), and economics (is the value worth the operating cost) — together.

Value realization

Translate technical and workflow improvements into outcomes leaders care about; decide whether to expand, revise, pause, or stop.

Capability → value
Capability→Workflow fit→Adoption→Behavior change→Measurable outcome

A working AI capability doesn't automatically create value — it has to fit a real workflow, get adopted appropriately, actually change behavior, and then show up in a measurable outcome. Any link in that chain can break.

Reference / deeper dive

Business & process economics

Revenue, gross/contribution margin intuition, fixed vs. variable cost, cost-to-serve, capacity, AI/inference and review labor, error cost, opportunity cost, ROI and payback.

Cost & ROI modeling

Connect technical choices to costs a customer actually cares about, including the human work that remains around the AI. Baseline labor cost, model/tool/platform cost at expected volume, human review and exception-handling cost, time/capacity benefit, break-even volume, and sensitivity to quality or review rate.

Success hierarchy, cross-reference

Adoption → quality → intervention → business outcome → economics. Introduced account-side — Stage 05 →

Practice

Build a baseline and measurement plan; critique a causal claim; identify missing instrumentation; write an expand / revise / stop recommendation. Model a simple pilot with baseline labor cost, AI cost per transaction, review time, adoption, and error/rework — show the break-even point and which assumption matters most.

07Quality, Risk & Responsible AIDefine good behavior, find meaningful failures, and decide what is safe enough to ship and operate.eval design · failure taxonomy · regression · HITL · monitoring · AI security · governance

Small-company core, now

Define good behavior before launch

Define what "good" looks like before rollout instead of waiting for users to say something feels off. Vague goals like "helpful" or "accurate" need to become behavior two reviewers would agree on: core cases, edge cases, ambiguous inputs, tool failures, safety cases, pass/fail thresholds and severity, correct source/tool use — not only a plausible final answer.

Eval design

Success criteria, representative workflow and user slices, core/edge/adversarial cases, false-positive versus false-negative consequences, graders, thresholds, and separating component quality from end-to-end workflow outcomes. Protect against overfitting to one fixed suite and add meaningful production failures as regression cases. Applied in Meridian →

The eval loop
Cases→Run→Score→Diagnose failures→Change→Rerun

The same cycle Meridian's Lab actually runs: pick representative cases, run them, score the results, diagnose what really failed, make one justified change, then rerun to confirm it worked.

Human evaluation quality

Write reviewer instructions and calibration examples, check agreement on ambiguous criteria, adjudicate disagreements, and verify that rules-based or model-based graders remain aligned with human judgment.

Failure analysis & regression

Taxonomies, severity, reproducibility, edge cases, trace review, regression suites, and checking whether an improvement creates new failures elsewhere.

Human review & guardrails

HITL placement, confidence/abstention, escalation, prohibited actions, permissions, verification, and designing review around consequence rather than blanket oversight. Includes confidence calibration — checking whether a stated confidence score actually matches the real correctness rate, not just whether a threshold exists.

Production quality

Monitoring, drift/input shifts, versioning prompts/models/knowledge, feedback signals, incidents, fallback/rollback, and continuous evaluation after launch.

PM / specialist depth, later

AI security & threat modeling

Identify who or what could cause harm and through which path: direct/indirect prompt injection, data exfiltration, insecure tool output, excessive permissions or agency, poisoned retrieval sources, secrets exposure, and misuse. Match layered controls to the likely consequence.

Responsible AI, governance & decision rights

Privacy, bias/fairness, risk classification, impact assessments, documentation, auditability, and explicit ownership for data, knowledge, eval criteria, risk acceptance, launch approval, incidents, exceptions, and retirement. Know when specialist, Security, Privacy, or Legal review is required.

Formal eval-program ownership

Large benchmark/golden-set design, grader calibration at scale, systematic model/prompt comparison, eval pipelines and CI gates — real PM/specialist territory, deferred deliberately. Meridian demonstrates the small-company core above; deeper eval work isn't the next priority.

Practice

Build eval cases, review traces, classify failures, set a human-review threshold, run a regression comparison, and write a short ship / do-not-ship recommendation. Build a compact eval plan with test categories, pass criteria, severity, expected source/tool behavior, and launch-blocking thresholds.

Communication practice

Explaining the pilot without overselling it

This mock stakeholder brief translates the operating design into a clear promise, explicit boundaries, and an evidence-based expansion gate.

Customer update · practice artifact
01

The pilot promise

What we are testing: an eight-week AI-assisted Tier 1 pilot intended to improve first-response time without weakening response quality or customer trust.

What changes: eligible low-risk tickets can be classified, grounded in approved knowledge, and drafted with AI. A rep reviews every response.

02

The boundaries

Money, security, and other higher-risk requests continue directly to a human. The pilot is not testing autonomous support.

I would not claim
  • That results have already improved.
  • That AI will solve the backlog.
  • That human review is temporary.
03

The expansion gate

How we judge it: response time, CSAT, grounding, escalation behavior, rep adoption, and failure patterns must be reviewed together.

Expansion would require faster eligible-ticket handling without degraded customer experience, unacceptable risk, or unmanageable review burden.

Evidence boundary

Meridian is fictional and this brief is practice material. AI helped me iterate on the wording; the portfolio evidence is the judgment behind what I emphasize, what I refuse to promise, and what proof I require before expansion.

Applied Work

Practice the capabilities in real problems.

This is the evidence layer of the learning project. Meridian goes deep on one end-to-end AI implementation; the Workbench goes wide through smaller cross-functional scenarios. The site itself is the portfolio. Applied Work lets you browse by case; Capabilities lets you browse the same work by capability.

One simulated B2B SaaS support implementation followed deeply through discovery, measurement, workflow design, guardrails, evaluation, adoption, operations, and value realization.

Breadth · transfer practice

Workbench · Smaller Cases

The Workbench is the collection of smaller Applied Work cases. These exercises test whether the same judgment transfers beyond Meridian into different workflows, business functions, industries, and levels of AI autonomy.

Use the Workbench when you want to see:

How I approach a new problem without assuming the answer is “use more AI.” Harborstone invoice operations is the first completed case.

Capabilities demonstrated in Harborstone: Discovery & Workflow · Measurement & Value · Systems & Guardrails · Evals & Quality

Future domains: business/operations exercises, agentic work, and a legal-AI adoption scenario that can combine general AI implementation skills with domain knowledge.

Two real, working tools built and used outside the simulations — plus the portfolio itself as an applied project. What generating, reviewing, and correcting AI-assisted work actually looks like when the result has to be genuinely usable and safe to publish.

How ambiguous personal requirements become usable tools through iterative scoping, implementation, testing, privacy review, and design correction.

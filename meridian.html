AI Support Workflow Exercise

Meridian is a fictional company. I used it to work out where AI should participate in a support team’s workflow, what software should enforce, and when a person should take over. The useful result was seeing how much the design changed once discovery challenged my first assumptions.

Simulated The company, stakeholder responses, and operating numbers. These were exercise inputs used to test my assumptions, not findings from real research.

What discovery changed

I started with a generic idea: AI drafts replies so support reps can answer faster. By the end of discovery, the design was narrower and more specific. Money, security and account-change requests skip AI drafting, and a separate check guards that boundary. A person reviews and sends every reply. The speed targets became modest. Two problems turned out to need configuration and access, not AI. All of this changed before prompting or model choice mattered.

Rep workflowHow do support reps actually handle first responses today?

What discovery showed: The simulated PM described a “mixed workflow”: most reps worked tickets start to finish, but on busy days some sent a quick acknowledgment first and came back later. My first-response target partly counted on that. When I asked the simulated reps directly, only 2 of 8 did it, and only occasionally. I gave their account of their own work more weight than the secondhand one.

What I decided: I revised the exercise’s first-response target from about 6 hours to about 7, against a baseline of more than 8. I also stopped expecting handling time to fall much, because a person still reviews every draft. Both are exercise targets, not measured results.

Billing and account contextDoes the AI need new integrations to see the account information reps use?

What discovery showed: Reps were copying billing and account status into tickets from a separate billing system. That field already reached the ticketing tool through a nightly sync, but only in the Tier 2 view. Turning it on for Tier 1 was a configuration change. It would help reps directly, and it would give the AI the same already-approved data without a new integration.

A real access gap: For questions that depend on a customer’s plan or setup, reps check the CRM record before answering. The AI would need separate read access to that data. Without it, an answer can be generically correct and still wrong for that customer. The exercise left this as a gap to close.

Unresolved: Because the sync runs nightly, billing data can be up to 24 hours old. The exercise flagged this but didn’t decide whether it’s current enough for billing questions.

High-risk categoriesShould AI still draft replies for money, account-access, and security issues if a person reviews them?

What changed: I decided those categories should bypass drafting entirely. Classification can route them, but the response itself starts with a person. The risk was not worth treating as a confidence-threshold problem.

Knowledge qualityWhat happens if the source material the AI retrieves is stale or contradictory?

What discovery showed: About 20% of the roughly 180 knowledge-base articles hadn’t been reviewed in over a year, and some conflicted. Reps’ search was basic keyword matching. Leadership kept the pilot timeline, so a cleanup couldn’t come first.

What changed: Articles would be fixed when a pilot failure was traced to them. Those failures would get their own root-cause tag, so bad source material wasn’t blamed on the model. I also stopped assuming semantic search was the answer and decided the retrieval options needed testing.

Support workflow and AI boundaries

The resulting design is deliberately bounded. AI helps with interpretation and drafting only where being probabilistic is useful. Software owns the hard stops. People remain responsible for anything consequential and for every message that reaches a customer.

What I would measure in a real pilot

I would not carry the exercise’s fictional target numbers forward. A real pilot would set baselines first, then track response and handling time alongside draft acceptance and editing, escalation reasons, knowledge-source failures, boundary violations, and customer outcomes. A faster workflow would not count as a success if reps spend the saved time correcting bad drafts or if risky categories leak through the boundary.

Testing the high-risk boundary

Classification can be wrong, so I didn’t want the decision to draft to rest on the category alone. The design adds a second check that reads the ticket text for money, security and account-change requests, whatever category the ticket was given.

A rules-based learning prototype included a rough version of that check. It used simple pattern matching, ran no AI model and handled no real customers. During the exercise it was rechecked against only a handful of cases. A later recheck of the saved prototype, in November 2026, found it caught some high-risk requests and missed three. One of the misses was the prototype’s own test case: “I need to change my admin password immediately” still got a draft. That test passed anyway, because it checked the ticket’s category, not whether a draft was produced. A passing test hadn’t shown that the boundary held.

A real pilot would have to measure how many high-risk tickets get past both checks, decide whether the check should lean toward over-escalating, test how uncertain tickets are routed, and confirm that confidence scores mean what they say. Mandatory human review is another checkpoint, not a guarantee that someone notices a misrouted ticket.

What the exercise showed

Several things that first looked like AI problems turned out to be workflow, access, source-quality, or policy problems, and the design changed before model choice or prompting became the important question. Simulated stakeholders sharpened that reasoning, but they cannot substitute for watching real people do the work, so real discovery would still come first.

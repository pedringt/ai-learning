Tastemake

I built Tastemake to explore a recommendation problem I kept noticing: broad labels can describe what someone consumes without explaining why they actually like it. The product learns from favorites, reactions, and exceptions to build a more useful picture of taste.

My role Product direction, UX, testing & final decisionsAI collaborator Research, design, engineering & QA supportStatus Working portfolio prototype
The problem

"You like fantasy" is technically true and often not very useful.

Recommendation products usually have plenty of labels, ratings, and similarity signals. What they often miss is the reason a person responds to something. Someone can love two fantasy books for completely different reasons and dislike a third that looks almost identical on paper.

I wanted to see whether a product could learn those more specific patterns without asking users to write a detailed profile from scratch.

What I built

A feedback loop that turns reactions into an inspectable taste model.

Favorites→Recommendations→React→Taste signals→Better discovery

Users can start from things they already love, browse when recall is difficult, react to recommendations, and inspect the patterns Tastemake is learning. Saved, tried, favorite, and negative reactions stay distinct because they mean different things.

FavoritesStrong positive evidence

Known loves give the system an initial signal without requiring a long questionnaire.

ReactionsPreference boundaries

More like this, less like this, and other feedback reveal what transfers and what does not.

Taste ProfileInspectable reasoning

Patterns are visible to the user instead of hiding all personalization behind a recommendation list.

LibraryIntent is not experience

Saving something for later stays separate from actually trying it and deciding how it landed.

The key product decisions

The product became less about recommending things and more about learning taste well.

Recognition over recallBrowsing became part of onboarding

Asking for four favorites sounds easy until someone cannot think of one. Browsing by category gives users another way to produce useful signals without turning setup into a test.

Not every preference is universalConditional patterns matter

I separated strong patterns from conditional ones so Tastemake can represent exceptions instead of pretending one reaction explains everything a person likes.

Explain the recommendation"Why this one?" is part of the product

The goal is not only to produce a plausible suggestion. Showing the reasoning gives the user something to judge, correct, or refine.

What changed

The first concept was a recommender. The stronger concept was a taste-learning product.

Earlier framing

Give Tastemake a few favorites and get recommendations.

Help people define their taste, then use that understanding to discover what might fit.

That shift changed the role of browsing, the Taste Profile, reaction design, and the difference between something saved and something actually experienced.

Where I landed

Personalization is more useful when the user can see and shape the model being built about them.

Tastemake gave me a practical way to work through cold start, feedback loops, subjective evaluation, and preference modeling. The next useful test is not whether the system can produce more recommendations. It is whether people recognize themselves in the patterns it learns and whether those patterns lead to discoveries they would not have found from broad categories alone.

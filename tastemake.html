Tastemake

Tastemake is a recommendation prototype for books, movies, TV, and games. It shows you what it thinks your taste is, so you can question it and correct it.

Status Working prototype. Not yet tested with outside users.

The problem

Recommendations didn’t understand why I liked something.

I used separate apps to track books, games, movies, and TV. Their recommendations often felt generic, and they rarely explained why I might like something. I wanted one place where I could see what the system thought my taste was, correct it, and have those corrections improve what it recommended next.

What I built

An editable model of personal taste

Tastemake looks at your favorites and ratings and proposes taste patterns across books, movies, TV, and games. It shows the titles behind each pattern, including evidence that doesn’t quite fit. As you rate more titles, it updates its understanding of your preferences rather than assuming every new rating confirms what it already believes. It doesn’t rewrite patterns you’ve confirmed.

You can confirm a pattern, reject it, say it’s only partly right, or limit it to certain contexts. Under “Partly,” you can mark titles that don’t fit, and the pattern stops citing them. Rejecting a pattern doesn’t erase the ratings behind it.

What corrections change

The visible Taste Profile, pattern history, and profile refreshes. Software enforces rejected patterns, areas you exclude from a pattern, and titles you say don’t fit it. After a “Not me” answer or an excluded area, titles Tastemake can recognize as matching that pattern are left out of your picks.

Where corrections have limits

Corrections help guide future recommendations, but they don’t change which titles Tastemake initially considers. It can exclude titles that match your corrections based on their catalog information, but some matches may still slip through, especially when that information is missing or incomplete. “Not sure” answers and additional context on a “Partly” answer still guide the AI rather than directly changing which titles are selected.

Saving a title you haven’t tried or marking it Not interested doesn’t count as evidence of taste. When AI is unavailable, recommendations fall back to catalog picks.

What testing revealed

A technically accurate explanation isn’t always a useful one.

I discovered that Tastemake sometimes explained a recommendation using a different preference than the one that actually drove its selection. I tried making explanations more consistent with the recommendation process by requiring them to reference the rating that influenced the choice. That worked technically. Explanations became much more consistent with the underlying scoring. But when I reviewed whether those explanations were actually specific and meaningful, the results got worse.

One example made the tradeoff clear. The original explanation connected a recommended game to a novel I’d liked for its sense of wonder and discovery. That felt like a meaningful connection. After the change, the explanation focused on a broad genre similarity instead. It was more consistent with the scoring process, but less useful in explaining why I might enjoy the game.

Matching the system’s internal reasoning isn’t enough. An explanation also needs to help someone understand why a recommendation might fit their taste.

Decision: I didn’t ship the rule. I kept the diagnostic trace and turned my attention to how candidates get selected.

What testing shaped

  • Honest feedback

    Labels and success messages describe only what the software does, including which corrections reach recommendations and when Live AI is in use.

  • Simpler onboarding

    Browse lets people find and rate titles they already know. Questions about why they liked something come after that first reaction.

  • Where recommendations still fall short

    Tastemake is better at recognizing broad similarities, like genre, than understanding the specific qualities that make someone love something. Even when someone explains why they enjoyed a title, the recommendation system doesn’t yet fully use that context to decide what to suggest. That can lead to recommendations that look similar on paper but miss what actually matters to the person.
A second example

A correction isn’t useful if the system loses what you meant.

Tastemake once grouped several very different kinds of comedy into a single taste pattern. They shared a broad genre, but the reasons I enjoyed them weren’t necessarily the same.

I first tested whether giving the AI better descriptions would help it recognize those differences. It didn’t. In a small review using my own preferences, none of the comedy comparisons were convincing. That led me to a different problem: people needed a way to correct Tastemake’s understanding of their taste.

I experimented with letting someone identify which parts of a pattern felt wrong and having AI rewrite the description. The prototype exposed another failure. In one test, a suggested correction sounded reasonable but quietly left out preferences I’d explicitly identified as important. The system appeared to accept my feedback without actually preserving its meaning.

So I chose a narrower approach that could be enforced reliably. In Tastemake today, someone can mark titles that don’t belong in a taste pattern, and the system removes those titles from that pattern’s supporting evidence. AI-generated rewriting remains experimental because the early tests showed it could misrepresent corrections. The Example Profile shows one of these corrections.

The rewriting experiment was an early prototype I tested seven times using manually prepared suggestions. It was not a released feature or an external-user study.

What’s next

Can understanding why someone liked something outperform genre matching?

The biggest open question is whether people other than me find the recommendations useful. I also want Tastemake’s sense of someone’s taste to shape which titles it considers, not just how it orders and explains them.

Tastemake

Tastemake is a recommendation prototype for books, movies, TV, and games. It makes its inferred taste patterns visible so people can question and correct them.

The problem

I wanted recommendations that understood why I liked something.

I’ve loved books, games, movies, and TV my whole life, but I used separate apps to keep track of them. In my experience, those apps mainly helped me log what I’d tried. Recommendations were often weak, and I couldn’t always tell why something had been suggested. I didn’t have a useful way to explain why I liked something or correct what the system inferred.

I didn’t want more recommendations. I wanted one place that showed what it thought my taste was, let me fix it, and carried those corrections into future recommendations across books, movies, TV, and games. That became Tastemake.

How it works

What you tell it, what it infers, and what a correction changes

What you supply. Favorites, and ratings of things you’ve actually tried: Loved it, Liked it, or Didn’t like it. Saving a title you haven’t tried, or saying you’re not interested, records intent. It never counts as taste evidence.

What Tastemake infers. A short list of taste patterns, such as “Atmosphere-driven horror in games.” Each one shows the titles cited as support, notes any that cut against it, and says how settled it is: emerging, supported, or strong.

How you correct it. For any pattern you can say it’s accurate, partly accurate, not you, or that you’re not sure. You can also say how much it matters, that it only holds in some contexts, or that it doesn’t apply to a category.

Correcting a pattern isn’t rejecting the favorite. Saying a pattern isn’t you rejects Tastemake’s reading of your ratings, not the ratings themselves. What you loved still counts as evidence.

Part of the productWhat a correction changes today (November 2026)
Taste ProfileYour answer is shown with the pattern, stored separately from Tastemake’s guess, and recorded in the pattern’s history. A correction never raises a pattern’s confidence, which comes only from what you tried.
Profile refreshThe AI that redrafts your patterns receives your answers. Software then drops any pattern you said isn’t you, rejects claims in a category you excluded, and caps how settled a pattern can be when you said it only holds in some contexts.
Choosing candidatesNo change. Software finds and scores catalog titles from your ratings only, so a title that resembles a pattern you rejected can still be recommended, because the original ratings still count. This is a current limitation.
AI ordering and explanationsThe AI that orders the six offered titles and explains each pick receives your answers and is told not to contradict them. That is an instruction; software doesn’t yet check that each pick follows it.
When AI is unavailableNo change. Picks come straight from the catalog shortlist.

Software enforces corrections in the Taste Profile. In recommendations, they currently guide the AI rather than bind it.

What my own testing changed

Testing made the product simpler.

Cold-start friction. Browse helps you find familiar titles and star the four favorites needed to get started. Ratings become taste evidence; simply viewing or saving a title does not.

Too much feedback at once. I moved deeper taste questions behind the first reaction. The card first asks whether you’ve tried something and how it landed; the questions about why only appear after that.

When the product promised too much. Reviewing the correction flow against the code revealed that Tastemake claimed some feedback changed recommendations when it didn’t. It also labeled catalog-generated picks as Live AI and sometimes reported changes that never happened. I separated what the software actually enforced from what it only asked the AI to do, corrected the misleading copy, and required the affected flows to be verified before release.

Evidence from the build

What I tested, and what it revealed

How the work is split today.

  1. Software retrieves real catalog candidates across books, movies, TV, and games, and removes anything you’ve already seen or ruled out.
  2. Software scores the rest against your ratings and shortlists six.
  3. A live AI call orders those six and writes a short explanation that cites something you’ve tried.
  4. Software checks that answer. If the call fails or the AI budget is used up, Tastemake shows the catalog shortlist instead and labels it as catalog picks.
  5. A second AI call drafts the Taste Profile’s patterns. Software keeps only cited titles you actually rated, limits how settled a pattern can claim to be by that evidence, and removes patterns you said aren’t you. It doesn’t check whether a cited title really fits the pattern.

I started conservative. Software decides what’s eligible and what counts as evidence; AI only orders and explains. That kept every AI answer checkable. Evaluation suggests that may also limit quality: the AI can only choose among the titles it’s given.

Experiment: give the AI more to choose from (November 2026, exploratory offline evaluation)

  • Hypothesis. Letting the AI choose six of eight candidates, instead of ordering a fixed six, would make recommendations follow corrections better.
  • What I measured. How often recommendations conflicted with a taste pattern the person had rejected, as classified in an offline evaluation, alongside other quality measures.
  • Result. In this exploratory evaluation, the change reduced those conflicts only slightly, and some other measures got worse. It wasn’t the fix it looked like.
  • What it revealed. The limited shortlist appeared to be one important constraint. In many cases, the AI couldn’t choose a suitable same-medium alternative from the eight titles offered. That pointed to candidate retrieval and scoring as problems worth investigating, rather than assuming a different AI ranking strategy would solve them.
  • What it doesn’t show. That the wider catalog lacked good titles, or that real users would prefer either version. These were exploratory offline results, not independently reproduced, and no outside users were involved.

Why the shortlist is weak. A follow-up diagnosis found that scoring leans on broad genre overlap, that blending scores with retrieval order can push higher-scoring titles off the shortlist, and that title synopses already in the catalog aren’t used by the scorer. None of these changes has shipped.

Latency. Waiting for recommendations became a product-design concern. I shortened the AI’s answers, sped up candidate retrieval, and revised the loading experience. In the 30 days ending November 3, 2026, the recommendation AI call took 3.3 seconds at the median and 4.5 seconds at the 95th percentile, measured across 49 successful calls. That excludes calls that failed and covers the AI step only, not the full wait from click to cards. I don’t have a comparable before figure.

Automated checks. Tests cover the evidence rules, AI output validation, profile corrections, accessibility, and layout at several screen widths. They show those rules hold. They don’t show that the recommendations are useful.

What I’d test next

Does knowing why beat matching on genre?

First, I want to find out whether knowing why someone loved a title produces better recommendations than matching on genre. Tastemake already asks for reasons. The next step is to let reasons and synopses shape which candidates are offered, make corrections reach that stage too, and rerun the same offline evaluation against the same measures.

Then, with outside users: do the picks appeal to them, and do their corrections make the next set better? No one but me has used Tastemake yet.

Where I landed

The editable taste model is the interesting part.

Tastemake makes its inferred taste patterns visible and lets people correct them. The hard part is making those corrections reach every stage that chooses what you see, and grounding recommendations in why people like things, not just what genre they are.

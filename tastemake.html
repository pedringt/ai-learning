Tastemake

Tastemake is a recommendation prototype for books, movies, TV, and games. It makes its inferred taste patterns visible so people can question and correct them.

The problem

I wanted recommendations that understood why I liked something.

I’ve loved books, games, movies, and TV my whole life, but I used separate apps to keep track of them. In my experience, those apps mainly helped me log what I’d tried. Recommendations were often weak, and I couldn’t always tell why something had been suggested. I didn’t have a useful way to explain why I liked something or correct what the system inferred.

I didn’t want more recommendations. I wanted one place that showed what it thought my taste was, let me fix it, and carried those corrections into future recommendations across books, movies, TV, and games. That became Tastemake.

How it works

What you tell it, what it infers, and what a correction changes

What you supply. Favorites, and ratings of things you’ve actually tried: Loved it, Liked it, or Didn’t like it. Saving a title you haven’t tried, or saying you’re not interested, records intent. It never counts as taste evidence.

What Tastemake infers. A short list of taste patterns, such as “Atmosphere-driven horror in games.” Each one names the titles that support it, notes any that cut against it, and says how settled it is: emerging, supported, or strong.

How you correct it. For any pattern you can say it’s accurate, partly accurate, not you, or that you’re not sure. You can also say how much it matters, that it only holds in some contexts, or that it doesn’t apply to a category.

Part of the productWhat a correction changes today
Taste ProfileYour answer is shown with the pattern and stored separately from Tastemake’s guess. The original pattern and its history stay visible. A correction never raises a pattern’s confidence, which comes only from what you tried.
Profile refreshThe AI that redrafts your patterns receives your answers. Software then drops any pattern you said isn’t you and limits claims you narrowed to some contexts or categories.
Candidate selectionNo change. Software chooses which catalog titles to offer from your ratings, not from your corrections.
AI ordering and explanationsThe AI that orders the offered titles and explains each pick receives your answers. It is told not to contradict them and to apply a partly accurate pattern narrowly. Software rejects a pick that tests a pattern you said isn’t you.

Whether a correction makes later recommendations better has not been measured.

What testing changed

Testing made the product simpler.

Cold-start friction. Browse helps you find familiar titles and star the four favorites needed to get started. Ratings become taste evidence; simply viewing or saving a title does not.

Too much feedback at once. I moved deeper taste questions behind the first reaction. The card first asks whether you’ve tried something and how it landed; the questions about why only appear after that.

Evidence from the build

What I tested—and what remains unproven

Software and AI have separate jobs. Software retrieves real catalog candidates across books, movies, TV, and games, removes anything you’ve already seen or ruled out, and picks the six that best match everything you’ve rated. A live AI call orders those six and writes a one-line reason that cites something you tried. Software checks that answer, and if the call fails it shows its own picks instead. A second AI call drafts the Taste Profile’s patterns, and software decides what counts as evidence and rejects patterns the evidence doesn’t support.

Latency. Waiting for recommendations became a product-design concern. I shortened the AI’s answers, sped up candidate retrieval and revised the loading experience, but I did not measure the resulting performance improvement.

Automated checks. Tests cover the evidence rules, AI output validation, profile corrections, accessibility, and layout at several screen widths. They show those rules hold. They don’t show that the recommendations are useful.

Unproven. Whether corrections improve later recommendations, and whether anyone other than me finds the recommendations better. Tastemake hasn’t been tested with outside users.

Where I landed

The editable taste model is the interesting part.

Tastemake shows the patterns behind its recommendations and lets people correct them. Those corrections reach the AI that orders and explains picks, but I haven’t yet shown that they make recommendations better, and no outside users have tried it.

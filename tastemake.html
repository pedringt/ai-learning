Tastemake

Tastemake is a recommendation prototype for books, movies, TV, and games. It makes its inferred taste patterns visible so people can question and correct them.

Status Working prototype. Not yet tested with outside users.

The problem

Recommendations didn’t understand why I liked something.

I used separate apps to track books, games, movies, and TV. Their recommendations often felt generic and rarely explained why a title might appeal to me. I wanted one place where I could see what the system inferred about my taste, correct it, and use those corrections to improve future recommendations.

What I built

An editable model of personal taste

Tastemake uses favorites and ratings to propose taste patterns across four types of media. Each pattern includes supporting titles, conflicting evidence, and an indication of how well-supported it is.

People can confirm patterns, reject them, qualify them, or limit them to certain contexts. Rejecting an interpretation doesn’t erase the original ratings.

What corrections change today

The visible Taste Profile, pattern history, and profile refreshes. Software enforces certain corrections, including rejected patterns and areas excluded from a pattern.

What corrections don’t yet control

Which catalog titles software retrieves and scores. The recommendation AI receives corrections as instructions, but software doesn’t yet verify that every pick follows them.

Saving a title you haven’t tried or marking it Not interested doesn’t count as evidence of taste. When AI is unavailable, recommendations fall back to catalog picks.

What testing revealed

A more accurate citation wasn’t a better explanation.

A diagnostic trace exposed a mismatch: Tastemake’s explanations often cited a different rating from the one that actually determined a recommendation. I tested a requirement that explanations cite the rating the scorer used to select the title.

For Breath of the Wild, the original explanation connected the game to Piranesi. That connection was rated specific and true. Requiring the deciding rating instead produced a genre-based comparison with Return of the Obra Dinn, which was rated incorrect.

The intervention improved alignment with the scoring trace but made some explanations less meaningful. The deciding rating was often a broad genre match rather than the most useful reason for recommending a title.

Decision: I didn’t ship the requirement. I kept the diagnostic trace and turned my attention to candidate selection.

What else changed through testing

  • Honest feedback

    Code review exposed claims that some corrections changed recommendations when they didn’t, plus misleading Live AI labels and success messages. I corrected the claims to match actual behavior.

  • Simpler onboarding

    Browse helps people find and rate familiar titles. Detailed questions about why they liked something appear after the initial reaction.

  • Candidate quality

    Scoring relies heavily on genre, retrieval order can affect the shortlist, and available synopses aren’t used by the scorer. A reason added to a Liked or Loved rating gives that rating more weight, but the scorer doesn’t use what the reason says. Richer title descriptions haven’t shipped. Their first held-out ranking test showed promise but failed its predefined success criteria, including a check against overgeneralizing dislikes.
What’s next

Can understanding why someone liked something outperform genre matching?

A first held-out test compared ways of ranking a fixed set of titles using richer descriptions and personal preferences. Structured matching showed promise but didn’t meet the experiment’s success criteria, and the sample couldn’t establish whether it improved film or TV recommendations. The bigger lesson was that knowing which part of a title someone disliked isn’t the same as understanding why. That’s the next problem to solve.

The larger unanswered question is whether outside users find the recommendations useful and whether correcting a Taste Profile improves their next set. The next challenge is making Tastemake’s understanding of taste shape which titles it considers, not just how it orders and explains them.
